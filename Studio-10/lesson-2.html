STUDIO 10 · LESSON 2
Two Ways to Tell It
One artist who built a story across thirty panels. One artist who packed a whole story into a single canvas. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
Does a story need many pictures, or can one be enough?
In Lesson 1 you saw that sequence and a single loaded image can both carry narrative. Now meet one artist who mastered each approach, and practice the four-step method for reading any story an image tells.
👀 PART 1 · A SERIES, NOT ONE PICTURE

Between 1940 and 1941, the American painter Jacob Lawrence made sixty small tempera panels telling one enormous story: the Great Migration, when millions of Black Americans left the rural South for cities in the North and West between roughly 1915 and 1970 in search of work and safety from Jim Crow laws. No single panel tells the whole story. Read together, in order, they do.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the museum site)
Jacob Lawrence, The Migration Series, 1940–41

Lawrence's sixty panels are under copyright and split today between two museums, so we view them at the source. Open a few panels in sequence and notice how flat, bold shapes and a repeated color palette carry the eye from one to the next, the way a single word repeated in a poem builds rhythm.

Jacob Lawrence at the Museum of Modern Art (holds thirty of the sixty panels)
All sixty panels of The Migration Series, on the Phillips Collection's Migration Series site

Lawrence gave each panel a short caption, but look at the panels themselves with the words covered: the story still reads through composition, color, and the repeated shapes of people in motion. He controlled what each panel showed and, just as carefully, what it left for the next panel to reveal.

📚 PART 2 · ONE IMAGE, A WHOLE STORY

Norman Rockwell worked in the opposite direction: one canvas, one moment, and a story you can reconstruct entirely from what surrounds that moment.

JL
Jacob Lawrence
American, 1917–2000
THE SERIES STORYTELLER

Lawrence trained in Harlem during the 1930s and became one of the first Black American artists to receive major gallery representation. He often worked in numbered series, planning every panel's palette and composition before painting any of them, so the sequence would hold together as a single work. The Migration Series remains one of the most studied visual narratives in American art.

NR
Norman Rockwell
American, 1894–1978
THE SINGLE-FRAME STORYTELLER

Rockwell painted magazine covers for decades, and a magazine cover gets one image to hook a reader. He became a master of loading a single frame with clues: a facial expression, an object left in the wrong place, a gesture mid-motion, so a viewer could reconstruct what just happened and guess what happens next, all without a caption.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the museum site)
Norman Rockwell, The Runaway, 1958, and The Love Song, 1926

Both are under copyright, so view them at their home museums. In The Runaway, notice everything you can infer just from posture and setting: who ran, who found him, and what will likely happen in the next five minutes, even though the canvas shows one instant.

Norman Rockwell, The Runaway, 1958, on the Norman Rockwell Museum's Illustration History site
Norman Rockwell, The Love Song, 1926, at the Indianapolis Museum of Art at Newfields
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Rockwell's device is called implied time: showing one frozen instant so clearly staged that a viewer's mind automatically supplies the moment before and the moment after. It is the single-image version of what Lawrence does across sixty panels.
🔍 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

A critique method reads any story-carrying image in a deliberate order: describe, analyze, interpret, judge. You may not skip ahead. Here it is applied to a single panel near the middle of Lawrence's series (imagine one showing a crowded train platform).

D · Describe (just the facts)

Flat, angular figures in dark coats crowd a narrow platform. Suitcases and bundles are stacked at their feet. The shapes repeat: same coat, same posture, many times over.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

Lawrence uses repetition and pattern: the repeated silhouettes turn individuals into a movement of thousands. Tight composition crams the figures to the frame's edges, so the platform itself feels too small for the crowd.

I · Interpret (what is it saying?)

This is not one family's story. Repetition insists that this moment happened again and again, to enormous numbers of people, at once personal and historical.

J · Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds precisely because it resists sentimentality. The flat, repeated shapes keep the panel from becoming about one dramatic face, and instead make it about scale and history.

⚡ TRY IT · SEQUENCE OR SINGLE IMAGE?
Read each description. Decide which storytelling approach it best matches, then check it.
"One painting shows a boy mid-step, glancing backward, a torn sleeve, while an adult behind him looks amused rather than angry."
"Sixty numbered panels, painted in order, follow families from a rural field to a crowded northern street."
"A single canvas shows a woman standing at an open door, coat half on, looking at a chair where a suitcase sits closed."
▶  In your notebook, write one Judge sentence about a Rockwell single-image work before you open this. Then compare.
A strong Judge sentence points back to evidence. For example: "The Runaway succeeds because Rockwell gives the viewer exactly enough detail, posture, setting, expression, to write the five minutes before and after the scene without a single word of text." Your wording will differ; what matters is that your verdict rests on what you described and analyzed.
✍️ NOW YOU TRY
Choose whichever artist pulls your eye more: Jacob Lawrence's Migration Series or Norman Rockwell's The Runaway and The Love Song, at the museum links above. In your notebook, run the same four steps, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, on the one you picked.
📝 VOCABULARY
SeriesA group of related artworks meant to be read together, often in order.
RepetitionRepeating a shape, figure, or color to build rhythm or emphasis across a work.
Establishing shotAn early panel that sets location and situation before action begins.
GestureThe pose or motion of a figure that suggests action or feeling.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Lawrence spread one story across sixty panels; Rockwell folded a whole story into one. Neither needed a caption. Both trusted a viewer to read change, gesture, and clue, and to finish the story themselves.
