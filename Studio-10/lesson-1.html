STUDIO 10 · LESSON 1
A Story With No Words
Pictures alone can carry a beginning, a middle, and an end. This Studio is about how. ~45 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How do you tell a whole story with pictures and no words?
Every image you have made this year has held one moment. This Studio asks something new: can your images hold time? Over two weeks you will build a visual story or sequential artwork, three to six panels, with no text anywhere in it.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Before any vocabulary, look at the photograph below: one horse, one stride, split into frames a fraction of a second apart. Read it left to right the way you would read a comic strip or a storyboard.

No single frame tells you what is happening. Laid out in order, though, you can read a beginning stance, a moment of full extension, and a return to the ground as one continuous action. That is the whole engine of visual storytelling: sequence plus change equals narrative, even when the "story" is a single stride.

💬 WONDER FIRST
Muybridge was not telling a story here; he was answering a scientific question (do all four hooves leave the ground at once?). Yet the sequence still reads as a tiny narrative: gather, launch, land. Hold that thought. This Studio's two featured artists build entire careers on the same principle, one working across many panels and one inside a single frame.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
When you look at a comic strip, a photo series, or a single busy painting, which do you usually notice telling a story faster?
📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

A narrative is simply a chain of events connected by cause and change. In visual art, artists build narrative two different ways, and this Studio teaches both.

📜 A SINGLE NARRATIVE IMAGE

  • One frame carries the whole story
  • Relies on implied time: what just happened, what is about to
  • Gesture, setting, and small clues do the work of many panels
  • The viewer's imagination fills in before and after

📋 A SEQUENCE OF PANELS

  • Multiple frames show time passing directly
  • Relies on sequence: establishing, action, resolution
  • Each panel is a fixed point; the story lives in the space between them
  • The viewer's eye is guided panel to panel

Either way, the artist is making the same core decision over and over: what to show, and what to leave for the viewer to supply. A story that shows everything is exhausting to look at. A story that shows just enough is the one that pulls you in.

THE ONE IDEA OF THIS STUDIO
Sequence and composition are how
pictures carry time without a single word.
Whether you work in panels or in one loaded frame, you are always deciding what the viewer sees, and what they must imagine.
NarrativeA story told through connected images or a single loaded image.
SequenceAn ordered series of panels that shows change over time.
PanelOne frame in a sequence; a single unit of a visual story.
Implied timeSuggesting what happened before or will happen after, inside one image.
🔍 PART 3 · WHY THIS COMES NOW

Every earlier Studio taught you a tool: composition, color, space, abstraction. Visual storytelling puts all of them to work at once, in service of a story that unfolds. Nothing you learned gets left behind; it all now serves a new problem: time.

Your project for these two weeks is a visual story or sequential artwork, three to six panels, with no text anywhere in it. No captions, no speech bubbles, no labels. The images alone have to do all the work.

▶  Before you read on, think of one small true story from your own life (getting somewhere late, finding something lost, a small argument) that has a clear beginning, middle, and end. Then open this.
Almost everyone can name one instantly, small stories are everywhere once you look for their shape. That is by design: the strongest projects in this Studio are rarely epic. A dropped cup, a missed bus, a shared umbrella; ordinary moments have a beginning, middle, and end just like the great sequences you will study. Keep your story in mind; it may become your project.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

A story does not need a single word to be understood. It needs a clear sequence, or one image loaded with the right clues, and a viewer willing to fill in the rest. For the next two weeks, that viewer's imagination is your collaborator.
