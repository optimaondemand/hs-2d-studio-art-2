STUDIO 10 · LESSON 3
Storyboarding & Sequencing
Every strong sequence is planned before it is drawn. Learn the three-beat structure professionals use. ~50 min + 30 min sketch
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How does an artist decide which moments in a story deserve their own panel?
Film directors, comic artists, and Lawrence himself all plan a sequence in rough form first, called a storyboard, before committing to any finished panel. Today you learn the same planning tool.
📋 PART 1 · THE THREE-BEAT STRUCTURE

Almost any short visual story, from a comic strip to a Migration Series section, can be broken into three beats. You do not need one panel per beat; you might spend two panels on action and one on resolution. But every beat should appear somewhere in your sequence.

BEAT 1
Establishing. Where are we? Who or what is here? The calm before anything happens.
BEAT 2
Action. The event. Something changes, moves, or arrives. The energy of the piece.
BEAT 3
Resolution. The aftermath. What is different now that was not true at Beat 1?

Muybridge broke one continuous motion into dozens of frames to study it scientifically. A storyboard does the opposite: it picks only the few frames, out of dozens of possible moments, that actually deserve to survive as panels. Both start from the same question: which instant belongs in the record?

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
A storyboard is a sequence of small, rough sketches made purely to plan pacing and composition, not to be finished art. Nobody but you needs to see your storyboard; its only job is to help you decide what belongs in each panel before you commit to your good paper or canvas.
⏳ PART 2 · CHOOSING YOUR MOMENTS

The hardest decision in sequencing is not how to draw a panel. It is deciding which moments earn a panel at all. A story with six panels devoted entirely to walking down a hallway will feel slow no matter how well each panel is drawn; a story that skips straight from calm to crisis with no establishing beat will feel confusing.

  1. List your story's moments. Write down every beat that happens, even small ones, in order.
  2. Mark the essential three or four. Which moments, if removed, would make the story stop making sense? Those survive. Cut the rest.
  3. Check your beat coverage. Does your surviving list include an establishing moment, at least one action moment, and a resolution? If a beat is missing, you likely need one more panel.
  4. Thumbnail each surviving moment. Small, fast, rough. Test whether each panel reads clearly on its own before you commit to a final composition.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH BEAT IS THIS?
Read each panel description from a hypothetical four-panel story about a dropped ice cream cone. Decide which beat it belongs to.
"A child holds a full ice cream cone, standing still on a sunny sidewalk, smiling."
"The same child sits on a bench, cone-less hands in lap, while a small dog happily licks a puddle on the ground nearby."
"The child trips on a curb; the cone flies from their hand mid-air, ice cream separating from the cone."
🎨 SKILL BUILDER 1 (completion-graded, in Canvas)

Storyboard It

Choose a small true or invented story with a clear beginning, middle, and end. Sketch a rough storyboard of four to six small boxes, thumbnail-quality only, labeling each with its beat (Establishing, Action, or Resolution). About 20 to 30 minutes.

Full directions and submission steps are in the Canvas assignment Skill Builder 1: Storyboard It.

📝 VOCABULARY
StoryboardA rough, quick sequence of sketches made to plan pacing and composition before finished work begins.
BeatOne distinct moment or event in a story's structure.
PacingHow quickly or slowly a sequence moves through its beats.
Establishing shotAn opening panel that sets location and situation before the action begins.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

A storyboard is cheap. A finished panel is expensive. Every minute spent planning which moments deserve a panel saves an hour of redrawing later. Plan the beats first; draw the finished art second.
