STUDIO 11 · LESSON 2
Type as Image
Two designers who made hierarchy and reduction into unforgettable design. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
What happens when the letters themselves become the picture?
In Lesson 1 you learned that design is art with a job: get a message across, fast. Now meet two designers who solved that problem in almost opposite ways, one by making type explode across the page, one by stripping an idea down to almost nothing. Then learn the four-step method critics use to read any designed work.
📚 PART 1 · TWO DESIGNERS, ONE HABIT

Both designers below are living or recently working, so their posters live at their museum and studio sources rather than reproduced here. Read the description first, then follow the link and give the actual work your full two seconds, then a slower second look.

PS
Paula Scher
American, b. 1948, living
TYPE AS IMAGE

Scher is a partner at the design firm Pentagram and one of the most influential graphic designers working today. Her poster for The Public Theater's Bring in 'da Noise, Bring in 'da Funk (1995) does not illustrate the show with a picture of dancers. It builds the entire image out of oversized, overlapping, hand-drawn type, packed edge to edge, radiating the show's energy through letterforms alone. For Scher, type is not just a message to read, it is the picture itself.

SB
Saul Bass
American, 1920–1996
REDUCTIVE, ICONIC DESIGN

Bass designed some of the most recognized film titles and posters of the 20th century, including Advise and Consent (1962) and the opening titles for films like Vertigo and Anatomy of a Murder. His method was the opposite of Scher's density: strip an idea down to one bold, simple symbol until nothing more could be removed without losing the message. A single torn shape or silhouette could carry an entire film's tone before a single frame played.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Scher and Bass represent two ends of the same skill. Scher builds hierarchy through density and scale contrast, type piled on type, sized to shout or whisper. Bass builds hierarchy through reduction, cutting until one shape or symbol carries the entire message alone. Both are disciplined choices, not opposites in quality.
🔍 PART 2 · WATCH IT WORK

You already know this critique order from earlier Studios: Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge, in that order, no skipping ahead. It works on design exactly as it works on a painting. Here it is applied to Saul Bass's approach to a film poster.

D · Describe (just the facts)

One bold, simplified shape or symbol fills most of the composition. A short film title sits in confident, geometric type. The color palette is tight, usually two or three colors.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

Bass relies on reduction and contrast. By removing every non-essential element, the single remaining shape becomes the hierarchy's top level automatically. There is nothing else competing for the eye's first stop.

I · Interpret (what is it saying?)

The poster is not summarizing the plot, it is setting a mood or tension in an instant, before the viewer knows anything else about the film. Reduction becomes a way of communicating feeling fast.

J · Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds because the design still reads clearly and communicates tone at a glance, decades later and at any size, from a small newspaper ad to a large poster. That durability is the mark of a strong hierarchy.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH CRITIQUE STEP?
Read each statement about a designed work. Decide which critique step it belongs to, then check it.
"The huge word fills the top third of the page while every other line of text is less than a tenth of that size."
"The poster still communicates its message clearly even shrunk to the size of a postage stamp, which is the real test of a strong hierarchy."
"There is one black torn-paper shape, one line of red type, and a large area of white space."
▶  In your notebook, write one Judge sentence about Paula Scher's poster before you open this. Then compare.
A strong Judge sentence points back to evidence. For example: "It succeeds because the dense, varied type sizes recreate the show's musical energy visually, so a viewer feels the rhythm before reading a single word." Your wording will differ; what matters is that your verdict rests on what you described and analyzed first.
✍️ NOW YOU TRY
Choose whichever work pulls your eye more: Paula Scher's dense, type-built poster or Saul Bass's reduced, single-symbol approach at the links above. In your notebook, run the same four steps, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, on the one you picked.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Scher piled type into density; Bass cut everything but one shape. Opposite methods, same discipline: both designers decided exactly what the eye would read first, and built everything else to support that decision.
