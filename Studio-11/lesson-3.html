STUDIO 11 · LESSON 3
Hierarchy & the Grid
The tools that decide what a viewer's eye reads first, second, and last. ~50 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Build visual hierarchy using scale, weight, and placement, and organize a page with a simple layout grid. Together they are how a designer decides, on purpose, what a viewer's eye reads first.
📚 PART 1 · THREE LEVERS OF HIERARCHY

Hierarchy is the order in which a design directs the eye through its parts, most important information first. A designer builds that order out of just a few levers, used on purpose rather than left to chance.

ScaleBigger reads first. A single dominant size difference does most of the sorting before anything else is considered.
James Montgomery Flagg, I Want You for U.S. Army, 1917. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
Weight & contrastHeavy versus light, dark versus pale, dense versus open. The eye is drawn to the strongest contrast on the page first.
Blythwood, Futura comparison, 2015. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.
Placement (the Z-path)In most Western reading, the eye scans top-left to top-right, then diagonally down to bottom-left, then bottom-right. Place your most important content along that path.
This is a real eye-tracking heatmap, shown here as genuine evidence of where eyes actually concentrate on a page, not a drawn schematic of the Z-path itself. Tschneidr, Eyetracking heat map Wikipedia, 2017. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.
💡 ONE DOMINANT ELEMENT, NOT THREE LOUD ONES
A common mistake is making everything big and bold at once. If every element competes for first place, hierarchy collapses and the eye has nowhere to start. Choose ONE thing to be the loudest. Everything else supports it.
📑 PART 2 · THE GRID KEEPS HIERARCHY HONEST

A layout grid is an invisible structure of columns, rows, and margins that a designer uses to place elements so the page feels organized rather than scattered. It is a tool, not a rule: once elements are placed with intention, some may deliberately break the grid to grab attention. But you have to build the grid first to break it on purpose.

A simple 3x3 gridDivide the space into thirds and let one dominant zone claim about two-thirds of it; the remaining cells hold supporting details. Hiroshige does exactly this, setting the bridge and figures low and to one side so the open thirds can carry the rain and sky.
Utagawa Hiroshige, Sudden Shower over Shin-Ohashi Bridge and Atake, 1857. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
Margins & alignmentConsistent margins on all sides and elements that align to shared edges make a page feel deliberate. Random spacing reads as sloppy, even with strong content.
Johannes Gutenberg, page from the Gutenberg Bible, c.1455. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
✍️ PART 3 · GUIDED PRACTICE (do this now)

Pick any short message: a made-up event, a reminder, a cause you care about. Work through all three thumbnails on one page.

  1. Thumbnail 1 (3 min). Sketch a tiny box representing your poster. Place ONE word or shape as the clear dominant element, huge, and everything else small.
  2. Thumbnail 2 (3 min). Same message, but sketch a simple 3x3 grid first, then place the same dominant element and supporting details inside grid cells, aligned to shared edges.
  3. Thumbnail 3 (3 min). Trace the Z-path with your finger on Thumbnail 2. Adjust one element so the most important information sits on that path.
⚡ TRY IT · DIAGNOSE THE LAYOUT
For each situation, decide which fix solves it best. Commit, then check.
A student's poster has three different words all in huge, bold type. A viewer says they do not know where to look first.
A poster's content is fine, but the margins are uneven and text blocks do not line up with each other, so it feels sloppy.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Scale, weight, and placement decide what the eye reads first. A grid keeps that decision organized instead of accidental. Choose one dominant element, build a grid to hold everything else, and hierarchy stops being luck.
