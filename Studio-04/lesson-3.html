STUDIO 4 · LESSON 3
One-Point Perspective
The ruler-and-pencil system behind Canaletto's canal. Keep paper, a pencil, and a straightedge beside you. ~50 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Set a horizon line, place a vanishing point, and draw orthogonals so that a simple room or street recedes into believable depth. This is the exact geometry that lets a flat page hold a space you could walk into.
📚 PART 1 · THREE PARTS, ONE RULE

One-point perspective is the simplest form of linear perspective. You use it when the scene faces you head on, so that all its depth lines run straight back to a single point. It has only three moving parts, and once you set them, the geometry does the rest.

Every one-point drawing has the same three parts:

  • The horizon line sits at the viewer's eye level. Set it high and you look down into the space; set it low and you look up.
  • The vanishing point is one point on the horizon. Every line that runs away from the viewer will head toward it.
  • The orthogonals are the receding edges. They connect the near corners of your objects to the vanishing point. Edges that face you straight on, top and side edges of a front wall, stay perfectly horizontal or vertical; they are not orthogonals.
💡 THE ONE RULE OF ONE-POINT
Anything running back into the space aims at the vanishing point. Anything facing you flat stays level (horizontals stay horizontal, verticals stay vertical). If a line is neither heading to the point nor staying level, it is almost certainly a mistake. That single test catches most beginner errors.
✍️ PART 2 · GUIDED PRACTICE (do this now)

Draw a simple one-point room. This is the same skeleton under a Canaletto street. Use a straightedge for every straight line.

  1. Draw the horizon and the point. Rule one horizontal line across the middle of your page: that is your horizon. Mark one dot on it near the center: that is your vanishing point.
  2. Draw the back wall. Draw a small rectangle in front of the point (it can overlap it). This is the far wall of the room. Its edges stay level, top and bottom horizontal, sides vertical.
  3. Connect the corners. Draw a light line (an orthogonal) from each corner of the back wall out to the four corners of your page, passing through the vanishing point behind. These become the floor, ceiling, and side walls.
  4. Add a door or a window. On a side wall, draw the near vertical edge of a door. To find its far edge, run its top and bottom back toward the vanishing point, then drop a vertical between them. It will automatically be the right shorter height.
  5. Firm up the true edges, erase the guides. Darken the real edges. Keep verticals vertical and level lines level. Lighten or erase the orthogonal guide lines you no longer need.
⚡ TRY IT · ORTHOGONAL OR NOT?
In a one-point drawing of a hallway that faces you head on, decide whether each edge is an orthogonal (heads to the vanishing point) or stays level. Commit, then check.
The line where the left wall meets the floor, running from the front of the hall to the back.
The top edge of a picture frame hanging flat on the far wall that faces you.
The row of ceiling lights running from just above your head toward the far end of the hall.
▶  In your notebook, draw the room through step 3 before you open this. Then check your setup.
Look for three things. First, is your back wall made only of level lines? If any of its edges tilt, redraw them straight. Second, do all four orthogonals actually pass through the single vanishing point? If they miss it, your walls will warp. Third, is the horizon a single straight line? A crooked horizon tilts the whole space. Fix those and the room will read correctly.

📝 VOCABULARY
Linear perspectiveA system for drawing depth using lines that converge on the horizon.
Horizon lineA horizontal line at the viewer's eye level.
Vanishing pointThe point on the horizon where receding parallel lines meet.
OrthogonalA receding edge that runs back toward the vanishing point.
One-point perspectiveLinear perspective with a single vanishing point, for scenes faced head on.
Two-point perspectiveLinear perspective with two vanishing points, for scenes viewed at an angle (a building corner).

💪 THE BIG IDEA

One-point perspective is not talent; it is geometry. Set a horizon, place one point, aim every receding edge at it, and keep the rest level. That single system built the streets and canals of Canaletto's Venice, and it will build the space in your project too.
