STUDIO 7 · LESSON 2
Painting Power and Story
Three artists, three sitters, three very different uses of symbol, pose, and setting. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
What does an artist decide about a sitter before a single brushstroke of the face is painted?
These three artists worked in different centuries and painted very different sitters, but each one used the same tools you learned to name in Lesson 1: symbol, setting, and pose. Because all three works are under copyright or held by living artists, you will view them at their museum sources rather than here.
👀 PART 1 · FRIDA KAHLO'S OWN SYMBOLS
FK
Frida Kahlo
Mexican, 1907–1954
THE SELF AS SYMBOL SYSTEM

Kahlo painted herself more than 55 times across her career, and almost never as a simple likeness. She surrounded her own face with a private, recurring vocabulary of animals, plants, and objects, each one carrying pain, identity, or belief. Her self-portraits are read like a language once you learn the symbols she returns to again and again.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the museum site) VERIFY URL AT DEPLOY
Frida Kahlo, Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace and Hummingbird, 1940

Kahlo's work is under copyright, so we view it at the source rather than reproduce it here. Study the necklace of thorns drawing blood at her throat, the dead hummingbird hanging from it, the black cat and monkey at her shoulders, and the flat leaf background. None of these are decorative. Look up what each one has been said to represent, then decide for yourself what the whole combination communicates.

Frida Kahlo, Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace and Hummingbird, 1940, at the Harry Ransom Center, University of Texas at Austin
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Kahlo lived with chronic pain and injury after a serious accident in her youth, and much of her symbolism grew out of that lived experience, along with her Mexican heritage and her political convictions. Her portraits are personal reporting, not decoration. That is the standard your own Studio Project will be held to: every symbol you choose should be true to you, not just visually interesting.
📚 PART 2 · AMY SHERALD'S QUIET STAGING
AS
Amy Sherald
American, b. 1973, living artist
GRAYSCALE SKIN, FULL-COLOR STORY

Sherald paints Black sitters with their skin rendered in grayscale, a deliberate choice that removes the usual visual cue of race and asks a viewer to look instead at pose, gaze, and the carefully chosen clothing and background color. Her most widely seen portrait is her official 2018 painting of former First Lady Michelle Obama for the National Portrait Gallery's America's Presidents collection.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the museum site) VERIFY URL AT DEPLOY
Amy Sherald, Michelle Obama, 2018

Sherald's work is under copyright, so we view it at the source rather than reproduce it here. Notice the seated pose, the direct and calm gaze, the sculptural volume of the dress, and the flat, unpatterned pale blue background. Nothing here is accidental staging. Ask yourself: what does the plainness of the background let the pose and gaze say more loudly?

Amy Sherald, Michelle Obama, 2018, at the National Portrait Gallery, Smithsonian Institution
🎭 PART 3 · KEHINDE WILEY'S REFRAMED POWER
KW
Kehinde Wiley
American, b. 1977, living artist
CLASSICAL POSE, NEW SITTER

Wiley borrows poses and formats directly from historical European portraits of aristocrats and rulers, then paints contemporary Black sitters into them, set against dense, ornamental, wallpaper-like patterned backgrounds. The strategy is not subtle, and it is not meant to be: he is asking who art history decided deserved to look powerful, and then deciding differently.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the artist site or a museum collection) VERIFY URL AT DEPLOY
Kehinde Wiley, Mrs. Siddons, 2012

Wiley's work is under copyright, so we view it at the source rather than reproduce it here. This painting reworks a famous 18th-century British portrait format. Look for the confident, theatrical pose borrowed from that older tradition, and the flat, busy floral pattern filling the entire background, pressing right up against the figure.

Kehinde Wiley, official artist site (search or browse for Mrs. Siddons, 2012)
💬 WONDER FIRST
Kahlo used private, personal symbols. Sherald used restraint and quiet color. Wiley used pattern and borrowed power. Three completely different strategies, the same underlying question. Which strategy feels closest to how you would want to represent yourself?
🔍 PART 4 · WATCH IT WORK · CRITIQUE ON KAHLO

Remember the order from earlier Studios: describe, analyze, interpret, judge. You cannot skip ahead. Here is that order applied to Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace and Hummingbird.

D · Describe (just the facts)

A woman faces the viewer directly against a flat background of large green leaves. A necklace of thorns encircles her neck, drawing small marks of blood. A dead hummingbird hangs from the necklace at her chest. A black cat sits at one shoulder, a monkey at the other.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

The composition is symmetrical and confrontational, the flat background pushes every symbolic object forward with no distraction, and the direct, unsmiling gaze refuses to soften what the objects show.

I · Interpret (what is it saying?)

Multiple sources connect this combination of thorns, blood, and a dead hummingbird to pain, entrapment, and a defiant refusal to hide suffering behind a pleasant face. The animals have been read as companions or as symbols pulling in different directions around her.

J · Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds because the symbols and the expression agree with each other. Nothing here is decorative; every object earns its place by adding to one honest statement about the sitter's experience.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH CRITIQUE STEP?
Read each statement about a portrait. Decide which critique step it belongs to, then check it.
"The dense floral pattern presses right up against the figure, leaving no empty space for the eye to rest."
"The sitter is seated, wearing a sculptural gray dress, against a plain pale blue background."
"By painting a contemporary Black sitter into a pose borrowed from an 18th-century portrait of nobility, the artist is questioning who has historically been shown as powerful."
▶  In your notebook, write one Judge sentence about Sherald's portrait before you open this. Then compare.
A strong Judge sentence points back to evidence. For example: "It succeeds because the grayscale skin and plain background force the viewer's attention onto the sitter's calm, direct gaze, which is exactly where the artist wants the sense of quiet authority to come from." Your wording will differ; what matters is that your verdict rests on what you described and analyzed.
✍️ NOW YOU TRY
Choose whichever portrait pulls your eye more: Kahlo's self-portrait, Sherald's Michelle Obama, or Wiley's Mrs. Siddons. In your notebook, run the same four steps, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, on the one you picked.
📝 VOCABULARY
PoseThe position and orientation of a sitter's body, chosen to convey mood, status, or character.
GazeThe direction and quality of a subject's eyes; direct, averted, calm, or confrontational.
ReframingDeliberately placing a subject into a format or tradition historically used for someone else.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Kahlo, Sherald, and Wiley solved the same problem three different ways: how do you make a viewer see more than a face? Symbol, restraint, and reframed power are three answers. Your Studio Project asks you to find your own.
