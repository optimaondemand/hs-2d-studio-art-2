STUDIO 7 · LESSON 6
Refine, Reflect & Present
A portrait earns its meaning in the finishing. Critique your own work, refine it, and present it well. ~55 min + studio time
🔍 PART 1 · CRITIQUE YOUR OWN PORTRAIT

Before you call it done, critique your own portrait using the same describe, analyze, interpret, judge order you used on Kahlo, Sherald, and Wiley in Lesson 2. Be your own first critic.

D · Describe

Step back. What does the portrait actually show? Squint: does the face read clearly, and are your symbolic objects and setting clearly visible from across the room?

A · Analyze

Check your tools. Is the proportion believable (re-check your thirds map)? Do your symbols and setting get the same rendering care as the face, or do they look rushed?

I · Interpret

Ask a friend, sibling, or family member who has not heard your plan to look at your portrait and guess what your objects might mean, without telling them first. Does their guess land close to your real intended meaning?

J · Judge

What one change would most improve it? Usually it is either strengthening a weak symbol, giving the setting more intention, or pushing the likeness closer to accurate. Make that change before you submit.

💭 REVISION CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
A viewer looks at your portrait and cannot tell why you included one of your objects. What is the most likely fix?
📝 PART 2 · WRITE YOUR ARTIST STATEMENT

Your artist statement travels with the artwork. It is two or three focused sentences, in your own voice, that show you made deliberate choices about identity, not just appearance. Type it into the submission text box with your portrait.

A strong statement answers three things:

  • Who is the sitter, and what objects and setting did you choose?
  • What does each symbol represent, specifically, about that person's story? (Name at least two symbols and their meaning.)
  • What surprised you in the process, or what would you do differently next time?
▶  Draft your three sentences in your notebook first, then open this for a sample of the register (do not copy it).
"I painted myself holding my father's old guitar pick and set against a plain warm background, the way Sherald keeps her backgrounds quiet so nothing competes with the sitter. I used sighting from earlier Studios to keep my proportions accurate, then gave the pick the same careful rendering as my face so it would not read as an afterthought. I was surprised how much choosing a plain background, instead of filling it, felt like its own strong decision rather than an easy one." Your statement should be this specific, but about your sitter and your choices.

You began this Studio asking how an artist paints who someone IS. Your finished portrait is your answer: a face built on honest proportion, surrounded by choices that are true to you specifically. That is what separates a likeness from a portrait.
