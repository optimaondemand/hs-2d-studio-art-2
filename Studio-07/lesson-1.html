STUDIO 7 · LESSON 1
Who Is This, Really?
A portrait can show a face and still miss the person. This Studio is about closing that gap on purpose. ~45 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How does an artist paint who someone IS, not just how they look?
Over the last six Studios you learned to see, arrange, color, build space, print, and layer meaning. This Studio turns all of it toward a single subject: a person. Not a photograph of a face, but a portrait that carries a story.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Spend a full minute with the painting below before you read anything else. It was painted in 1434, and scholars still debate exactly what it means, which is itself proof of how much a portrait can carry beyond a likeness.

Scholars have read meaning into nearly every object here: the little dog (fidelity), the single candle burning in daylight, the oranges on the sill (costly imported fruit, a sign of wealth), the shoes set aside as if the floor were sacred ground, and the small round mirror reflecting the whole room, including two more figures standing where the viewer stands. This is not a photograph. It is a room built entirely out of decisions.

💬 WONDER FIRST
If you were painted right now and could place exactly three objects near yourself, which three would you choose, and what would each one tell a stranger about you that your face alone cannot? Hold your answer. This Studio ends with you making that exact choice for real.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Think of a photo of yourself that you would actually want to represent you to someone who has never met you. What makes it the right one?
📚 PART 2 · LIKENESS IS ONLY THE FLOOR

Likeness is how closely a portrait matches a person's actual appearance. It matters, and Skill Builder A in this Studio will train it directly with a facial proportion map. But likeness by itself is a floor, not a ceiling. A technically perfect likeness can still tell a viewer nothing true about who the sitter is.

What lifts a portrait past likeness is everything the artist adds around the face on purpose: the sitter's pose, the setting they are placed in, the objects (called attributes) they hold or are surrounded by, and any pattern or iconography woven through the background. Each of these is a decision, and every decision is a chance to say something about identity.

📷 A LIKENESS PORTRAIT

  • Accurate facial proportion and features
  • Neutral or generic background
  • No chosen objects or setting
  • Answers only: what did this person look like?

🎭 A SYMBOLIC PORTRAIT

  • Accurate facial proportion, used as the foundation
  • A setting chosen to say something
  • Objects and pattern chosen to carry meaning
  • Answers: what did this person look like, value, and carry?
THE ONE IDEA OF THIS STUDIO
A face shows what someone looks like.
A symbol shows what someone carries.
Every technique in the next five lessons builds toward using both at once.

Meet the vocabulary you will use for the rest of this Studio. You will need every one of these terms to plan your own portrait in Lesson 5.

PortraitAn artwork representing a specific person, usually focused on the face and upper body.
Self-portraitA portrait an artist makes of themselves.
LikenessHow closely a portrait's features match the sitter's actual appearance.
Symbol / attributeAn object included in a portrait to represent an idea, value, role, or part of the sitter's story.
IconographyA system of symbols and their agreed meanings, used and reused across artworks.
SettingThe place or background a sitter is shown in, chosen to add meaning.
🔍 PART 3 · WHY THIS COMES NOW

Studio 6 taught you that unrelated fragments can add up to a single meaning through juxtaposition. Studio 7 asks a harder version of that question with a single, unified image: a portrait. You are not combining scraps anymore. You are choosing, with total control, exactly what surrounds one person's face, and every choice you make will be read as meaningful whether you intend it or not.

Your project for these two weeks is called The Symbolic Self. You will create a portrait, most likely a self-portrait, that uses objects, setting, and symbol to tell its sitter's story, not just their face. Two skill builders get you there: facial proportion (Lesson 3) and symbolism and setting (Lesson 4).

▶  Before you read on, name one object you own that you would want in your own portrait. Then open this.
Most people can name one instantly: an instrument, a piece of jewelry, a sport's equipment, a book. Hold onto it. It may become one of the three symbolic objects in your Studio Project. Notice that you already do the thing this Studio is teaching: you already know your object means something. The Studio will teach you how to make a viewer feel that too.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

A camera can capture a likeness in a fraction of a second. It cannot decide what a person's story is. That decision, what to include, what to leave out, what object to place in the frame, is the artist's alone, and it is what turns a picture of a face into a portrait of a person.
