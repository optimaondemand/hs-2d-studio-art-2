STUDIO 3 · LESSON 5 · WEEK 2 BEGINS
Plan & Create
You have the color tools. Now choose one subject, design three palettes, and begin. ~60 min + studio time
📌 PART 1 · CHOOSE ONE SUBJECT

Your Studio Project is Three Moods, One Subject: three versions of the same subject, in any color media, where color alone makes each version feel different. Choose something simple enough to repeat and interesting enough to hold color.

🏷️ GOOD SUBJECTS SHARE THREE TRAITS
It is simple to repeat (a single object, a tree, a window, a face, a still-life of two or three things), it has enough area to hold color (avoid tiny, thin subjects that leave no room to fill), and it is emotionally open, so it can believably feel joyful in one version and somber in another. The same subject each time is the whole point; do not change the subject to fit the mood.
💭 PLANNING CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Which kind of subject are you leaning toward?
🎨 PART 2 · DESIGN THE THREE PALETTES

Colorists plan the palette before they touch the final work. You will name three distinct moods, then build a small swatch set for each using the tools from Lessons 3 and 4. Here is an example plan for one subject. Yours will use your own moods and colors.

Mood 1: JoyfulWarm, high-intensity, analogous. Complement used only as a small accent.
Mood 2: CalmCool, mid-intensity, analogous. Light-to-mid values keep it gentle.
Mood 3: SomberDark, low-intensity, cool-leaning. Dulled hues carry the weight.
  1. Name your three moods (about 5 min). Pick three feelings that pull in clearly different directions, like festive, peaceful, and uneasy. Write each at the top of a swatch box.
  2. Build a small palette per mood (about 15 min). For each, mix or select three to five colors. Decide the harmony (complementary, analogous, monochromatic), the temperature, and set value and intensity to match the mood.
  3. Draw the subject once, lightly, three times. Same subject, same composition, three copies. Keep the drawing identical so only color changes.
  4. Color each version from its planned palette. Resist reaching for "the real" color of the subject. Trust your palette. Work the whole surface so no version is left half-colored.
💡 KEEP THE DRAWING THE SAME
The experiment only works if the three versions are the same subject in the same arrangement. If the shapes change too, viewers cannot tell whether the new feeling came from color or from the redrawing. Lock the drawing, then let color do all the talking.

🥽 VR Session · Repaint the Room

Week 2 · about 25 minutes · Works for a solo student; a live class runs it unchanged. This is a completion-graded activity; full submission steps are in the Canvas assignment VR Session: Repaint the Room.

  • 1Before you enter: have your three mood words and the color vocabulary ready. Enter your VR space and load the Studio 3 gallery scene from Canvas. You may choose any gallery Location you like.
  • 2Observe (about 5 min): stand in the scene and note its current palette. Is it warm or cool, bright or dulled, light or dark? What mood does it currently give?
  • 3Repaint (about 12 min): use the VR color and paint tools to recolor the scene twice: once toward a joyful mood and once toward a somber one. Change only color, not the objects.
  • 4Reflect (about 5 min): answer aloud or in writing: "Which color changes did the most work to shift the mood, and why?" In a live session, share and respond to one classmate.
  • 5Exit: capture a screenshot of each repainted version and upload them. No screenshot tool? Write two or three sentences describing the color changes you made and their effect.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND COLOR CHOICES
An AI image generator can recolor a picture into "a happy version" or "a sad version" in seconds, and some tools can suggest palettes. What it cannot do is make the color decisions that this project is grading: understanding why a low-intensity cool palette reads as mournful, and choosing your three moods and every hue, value, and intensity to serve them. The reasoning behind each palette, and the three artworks themselves, must be your own work. If you look at any tool for palette ideas, say so in your artist statement and explain the choices you made yourself.
