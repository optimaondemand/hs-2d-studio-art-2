STUDIO 3 · LESSON 2
Van Gogh and the Fauves
Three artists who let color leave reality behind. Then a tool for reading color in any work. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
What happens to a painting when an artist stops copying the color of things and starts choosing it?
In Lesson 1 you felt color work on you. Now watch three artists use it on purpose, and learn the four-step method critics use to read a work. You will run that same method on your own paintings in Lesson 6.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Van Gogh painted his sunflower canvases in Arles, in the south of France, while waiting for his friend Paul Gauguin to arrive. He wanted the room to glow. Look at how little variety of hue he uses here, and how much feeling he pulls out of it.

Almost the entire painting lives in one family of color: yellow. Van Gogh set bright lemon petals against golden-brown centers and a pale yellow-green wall. Because the hues are so close, they harmonize rather than fight, and the whole canvas seems lit from inside. This is a monochromatic and analogous choice, and it is a decision, not an accident of what sunflowers "look like."

📚 PART 2 · THREE ARTISTS WHO FREED COLOR

These three worked in different decades and different styles, but each made the same radical move: color no longer had to match the object. It could serve the feeling instead.

VvG
Vincent van Gogh
Dutch, 1853–1890
THE EMOTIONAL COLORIST

Van Gogh pushed color past what he saw to what he felt. He once wrote that he wanted to use color to express emotion rather than to copy nature exactly. His fields blaze yellow, his night skies turn deep blue, and his complementary pairings, yellow with violet, blue with orange, make the surfaces seem to vibrate. Color, for him, was the voice of the picture.

HM
Henri Matisse
French, 1869–1954
THE FAUVE

Around 1905 Matisse and a group of painters used color so bold and unnatural that a shocked critic called them les fauves, "the wild beasts." A face might be split by a green stripe; a background might be pure hot pink. The point was not accuracy. It was that a strong color relationship could carry more truth about a person or a place than a literal one.

MR
Mark Rothko
American, 1903–1970
THE COLOR-FIELD PAINTER

Rothko removed the subject entirely. His mature paintings are large soft-edged rectangles of color stacked on a colored ground. With no figure and no object, color itself becomes the whole experience: some canvases feel warm and glowing, others solemn and heavy. He wanted viewers to stand close and be surrounded. His work is under copyright, so we view it at the museum.

Here is the painting that got them the name. Give it a full minute before you read on.

This is Matisse's wife, Amelie. Look at what he did to her face: a green stripe down the center, yellow on one cheek, violet in the shadows, a slash of red at the mouth. Not one of those is a color human skin actually is. The background behind her is not a room; it is a collision of green, violet, and orange with no object attached to any of it. When this hung at the 1905 Salon d'Automne, viewers were genuinely angry, and one critic's insult, les fauves, became the name of the movement.

Now the part worth sitting with: despite every "wrong" color, she still reads as a specific, living, self-possessed person. The colors are false and the portrait is not. That is the whole argument of this lesson in one canvas.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Fauvism was a short-lived movement (roughly 1905 to 1908) whose painters used intense, non-naturalistic color for expressive effect. It was one of the first modern styles to treat color as free from the job of describing objects, an idea that runs straight through to Rothko fifty years later.
🔍 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

Critics read a work in a deliberate order so that judgment is earned, not blurted: describe, analyze, interpret, judge. The rule is simple: you may not skip ahead. Describe before you analyze; analyze before you interpret; interpret before you judge. Here is that critique process applied to Van Gogh's Sunflowers above, with the focus on color.

D · Describe (just the facts)

A bunch of sunflowers stands in a rounded earthenware vase against a pale wall. Nearly everything is yellow: bright lemon petals, golden-brown centers, a soft yellow-green background. The paint is thick and textured.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

The palette is analogous, built from neighboring warm hues, with the drama coming from shifts in value and intensity rather than from many different colors. The darker seed centers anchor the bright petals; the muted wall lets the flowers stay the loudest thing in the room.

I · Interpret (what is it saying?)

The relentless warmth reads as light, life, and hopeful energy. By pouring everything into one glowing hue family, Van Gogh makes ordinary cut flowers feel almost radiant, as if the picture itself gives off heat.

J · Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds because the limited palette is the point. A more "realistic" range of colors would have scattered the effect. The discipline of staying in one hue family is exactly what makes the painting glow.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH CRITIQUE STEP?
Read each statement about a colorful artwork. Decide which critique step it belongs to, then check it.
"The orange figure pops forward because it sits against a field of its complementary blue."
"The top half of the canvas is deep red and the bottom half is dark brown."
"The cold blues make the empty room feel lonely, as if no one has been there in a long time."
▶  In your notebook, write one Judge sentence about Van Gogh's Sunflowers before you open this. Then compare.
A strong Judge sentence points back to evidence. For example: "It succeeds because staying inside one yellow family, rather than using many colors, is exactly what lets the flowers glow." Your wording will differ; what matters is that your verdict rests on what you described and analyzed, not on "I just like it."
✍️ NOW YOU TRY
Choose whichever work pulls your eye more: Van Gogh's Sunflowers above, Matisse's Woman with a Hat, or Rothko's Painting at the museum links. In your notebook, run the same four steps, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, on the one you picked.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Van Gogh, Matisse, and Rothko share one move across seventy years: color does not have to obey the object. It can obey the feeling. The critique process is how you slow down enough to see the color decisions behind that feeling, in their work and soon in your own.
