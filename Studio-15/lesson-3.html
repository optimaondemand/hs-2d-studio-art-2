STUDIO 15 · LESSON 3
Structured Experimentation
A method for turning curiosity into evidence. Have your materials scrap box ready. ~50 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Design a fair experiment by naming one variable and holding everything else as a control, then document a trial honestly, including a trial that fails. This is the method behind every entry in your Studio Project's experiment log.
📚 PART 1 · ONE CHANGE AT A TIME

An experiment only teaches you something if you can point to exactly what caused the result: separate what you are testing from everything you are keeping the same.

🔄 THE VARIABLE (change ONE thing)

The single factor you are curious about: a new material, an unusual order of layers, an amount of water, a tool used the "wrong" way. This is the question you are asking with your hands.

📋 THE CONTROL (hold everything else steady)

Paper, surface size, drying time, light, and every other technique stay exactly the same as your last trial. If three things change at once, you will not know which one caused the result.

Here is the method in practice. Suppose your question is: what happens if I draw with ink over paint that is still wet, instead of dry? The variable is whether the paint is wet or dry when the ink goes on. Everything else, the paper, the ink, the amount of paint, the brand of paint, stays fixed across both trials. Only then can you trust the comparison.

💡 WHY "JUST TRY STUFF" DOES NOT TEACH YOU MUCH
If you change the paper, the paint, the tool, and the drying time all in the same trial, and something interesting happens, you will not know which change caused it. You will not be able to repeat it on purpose, only by luck. One variable at a time is slower, but it is the only way to actually learn the material's behavior.
✍️ PART 2 · GUIDED PRACTICE (do this now)

Use your materials scrap box from Lesson 1. You are going to run and document three small trials on scrap surfaces, not finished paper. This practice log is a rung, not the final Studio Project.

  1. Pick one pairing (2 min). Choose two techniques or media from different Studios this year that you have never combined, for example ink over dry pencil, collage under a wash of paint, or printmaking ink combined with charcoal.
  2. Name your variable (2 min). Write down, in one sentence, the exact thing you are testing. "What happens if I apply watercolor over a graphite drawing before the graphite is sealed?"
  3. Name your controls (2 min). List what will stay the same across every trial of this pairing: same paper, same amount of pencil pressure, same brush, same amount of water.
  4. Run trial 1 on a scrap (10 min). Do it. Watch closely while it happens, not just after.
  5. Log the outcome honestly (5 min). Write exactly what happened, even if it failed or looked messy. A failed trial recorded honestly is more useful to you than a success you cannot explain.
  6. Run two more trials, changing only the variable (15 min). For example, try the same pairing with more water, then with less. Log each one the same way.

Three-Trial Experiment Log

Using the log template above, run and document three trials of one media pairing on scrap surfaces (photograph or scan each scrap next to its written log entry). Name your variable and your controls clearly for all three, and be honest if a trial did not work. This is a completion-graded process rung, not a finished artwork.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

One variable, steady controls, and an honest log turn a lucky accident into a repeatable discovery. That structure is what makes an experiment different from a mess, no matter how wild the materials get.
