Studying Innovation
You have already studied artists who broke rules on purpose. Revisit two of them with new eyes. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
If an artist had followed the accepted rule, what would we have lost?
This Studio has no new featured artist. Instead, you are invited to look back at any artist from an earlier Studio through a new lens: not "what technique did they teach me" but "what did they risk to get there." Two examples below are optional springboards, not assignments. You may revisit either, both, or a different artist from this year entirely.
📚 PART 1 · TWO ARTISTS WHO RISKED THE ACCEPTED RULE

Every artist you studied this year had a known rulebook available to them, and every one of them broke it somewhere. Two examples from earlier Studios are worth a second look now, through the lens of risk rather than technique.

HH
Hannah Höch
German, 1889–1978 · met in Studio 6, Layers of Meaning
RISKED: COMBINING WHAT WAS NOT SUPPOSED TO GO TOGETHER

In Studio 6 you studied Höch as a pioneer of photomontage, cutting and combining printed photographs into new images. Look again: in her time, cutting up mass-produced magazine photographs and gluing the pieces into fine art was not an accepted studio practice. It looked more like scrapbooking than painting to critics who expected oil on canvas. Höch's risk was combining a "low" material (found print) with "high" artistic ambition, and trusting that the combination itself could carry meaning no single material could carry alone.

GO
Georgia O'Keeffe
American, 1887–1986 · met in Studio 9, Transformation
RISKED: PUSHING ONE IDEA PAST WHERE IT WAS COMFORTABLE

In Studio 9 you studied O'Keeffe magnifying and simplifying natural forms toward abstraction. Her risk was not a new material; it was refusing to stop at the point where a botanical illustration would have stopped. She kept cropping closer and simplifying further than convention expected of a flower painting, trusting that the abstraction would still read as the form underneath. That is the same habit this Studio asks of you: take a known move and keep pushing it one step past where you would normally stop.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Neither artist above changed materials to be different for its own sake. Each pushed on a real limitation they had noticed in the accepted approach of their time. The best experiments in your own log will come from the same place: something you noticed felt limited, and a real curiosity about what would happen if you pushed past it.
🔍 PART 2 · WATCH IT WORK

The four-step critique order from earlier Studios still applies here: Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge. Applied to an experimental work, the Judge step changes slightly: instead of only asking "does it succeed," you are also asking "was the risk worth taking." Here is that critique method run on Höch's combination of photograph and glue.

D · Describe (just the facts)

Cut fragments of printed photographs, arranged and glued onto a single surface. Some edges are cut cleanly; others are torn. Scale is inconsistent: a large hand might sit beside a tiny face.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

The composition relies on juxtaposition, unrelated images placed together, and deliberately mismatched scale, which creates visual tension instead of the harmony a traditional composition would seek.

I · Interpret (what is it saying?)

The combination suggests that meaning is not fixed; it is assembled, the same way a modern reader assembles meaning from scattered printed images and headlines rather than one coherent story.

J · Judge, including the risk (does it succeed, and was the risk worth it?)

It succeeds, and the risk is exactly why. A "safer" version, painted rather than cut-and-glued, would have lost the very thing the work is about: that meaning today comes from combination, not from one clean, single source.

⚡ TRY IT · RISK OR SAFE CHOICE?
Read each studio decision. Decide whether it is an experimental risk (an unproven variable) or a safe, already-proven choice. Commit, then check.
An artist pours diluted ink onto paper that has already been painted with wet watercolor, to see how the two wet media interact before either dries.
A student who has shaded dozens of spheres this year shades one more sphere, using the same graphite and the same five light zones as always.
An artist layers torn magazine collage under a coat of melted crayon (encaustic-style), applying heat to see how the wax interacts with the paper and ink beneath it.
▶  In your notebook, name one Studio from this year and one rule it taught you. Then open this.
There is no single correct answer here; the exercise is the noticing. For example, Studio 3 taught that color choices should build a coherent mood, and Studio 11 taught that a design's hierarchy should guide the eye clearly. Both are also rules you are now free to bend on purpose in this Studio, as long as you document what happens when you do.
✍️ NOW YOU TRY
Choose whichever artist pulls your interest more: Höch's photomontage at the museum link above, or O'Keeffe's magnified natural forms from Studio 9. In your notebook, run the same four steps, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, including whether the risk was worth taking, on the one you picked.
📝 VOCABULARY
InnovationA genuinely new combination or use of materials and techniques, arrived at through deliberate risk.
HybridA work that combines two or more distinct media or techniques in one piece.
RiskA studio choice with a genuinely unknown outcome, as opposed to a well-practiced, predictable one.
SpringboardAn earlier artist's idea used as a starting point for your own, different experiment.
Mixed mediaArtwork made by combining more than one material or technique in a single piece.

💪 THE BIG IDEA
