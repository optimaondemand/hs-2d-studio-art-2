STUDIO 5 · LESSON 4
Carve, Ink, Register
The physical process behind every relief print, from Hokusai's workshop to your kitchen table. ~55 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Explain how carving creates a relief print, ink a plate correctly, use registration to line up a print the same way every time, and understand what an edition is. This is the process that will carry your motif from Lesson 3 into an actual print.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT MAKES A PRINT A "RELIEF"

A relief print works by removing material, not adding it. You carve away the parts of a plate you want to stay white (unprinted), and the parts you leave raised are what picks up ink and touches the paper. It is almost the reverse of drawing: instead of adding marks where you want ink, you cut away everywhere you do not.

This is exactly what Hokusai's carvers did to his wave design, and exactly what you will do to your own motif, whether you carve a purchased foam printing plate, press lines into a foam tray with a dull pencil, or cut a stamp from an eraser or a potato. The tool changes; the logic (raised prints, carved-away does not) stays the same.

🔧 PART 2 · CARVE, INK, REGISTER, REPEAT

Four steps take a plate from blank to a finished edition. Skip any one of them and the whole print series suffers.

  1. Carve. Transfer your motif to the plate (trace or press it in), then remove or press down everywhere you want to stay unprinted. On foam, a dull pencil or stylus presses grooves in rather than cutting through; on a stamp, you cut away the negative space.
  2. Ink. Roll a thin, even layer of ink (or thick tempera/acrylic with a touch of dish soap) across the raised surface with a brayer or the back of a spoon. Too much ink fills your carved lines and blurs the motif; too little skips and looks patchy.
  3. Register. Line the paper up against the plate the exact same way every single time, using a fixed edge, a taped guide, or matching corner marks. Registration is what keeps a whole pattern or edition consistent instead of drifting print to print.
  4. Pull and repeat. Press evenly, lift carefully, and set the print aside to dry. Re-ink and register again for the next print. Every print pulled from this one plate belongs to the same edition.
💡 WHY REGISTRATION MATTERS FOR PATTERN
If you are printing the same motif across a page to build a pattern, poor registration is the single most common way a promising design falls apart: motifs drift crooked, overlap unevenly, or leave gaps that were not planned. A simple taped corner guide or a consistent grid mark solves most of it.
🔁 PART 3 · WHAT IS AN "EDITION," EXACTLY

Printmakers number their prints to record their place in an edition, written as a fraction like 3/10: this is the third print pulled, out of a planned total of ten. The number does not rank quality; a plate begins to wear down after many pulls, so early and late prints in a large edition can look slightly different, but each one is still considered a genuine, complete work, not a copy of some "original."

▶  Before you open this, guess: why might a printmaker deliberately keep an edition small instead of printing as many as possible?
A few real reasons: plates wear down and later prints can lose crispness, a smaller edition can make each print feel more considered, and for many printmakers the point is control, knowing exactly how many exist and in what condition, rather than maximizing output. There is no single correct edition size; it is a decision the printmaker makes on purpose, the same way you will decide how many prints your own series needs.

📝 VOCABULARY
Relief printA print made by carving away material so only the raised surface picks up ink.
Block (plate)The carved surface, wood, foam, linoleum, or another material, used to print an image.
RegistrationLining up the paper against the plate the same way every time.
EditionThe full numbered set of prints pulled from one block.
BrayerA roller used to spread an even layer of ink across a plate.
