STUDIO 5 · LESSON 2
What Repetition Says
Two very different artists who made the multiple itself the point. Then a close read of Hokusai's wave. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
Why would an artist choose to repeat an image instead of making something new each time?
In Lesson 1 you met the difference between the unique and the multiple. Now look at two artists who built careers on repetition, then practice DAIJ criticism, the four-step method, on the print that opened this Studio.
📚 PART 1 · TWO ARTISTS, ONE STRATEGY

Repetition is not just a printing shortcut. In the right hands it becomes the artwork's whole argument. Compare two twentieth-century artists who used repeated images to say something they could not have said with just one image.

AW
Andy Warhol
American, 1928–1987
THE FACTORY OF IMAGES

Warhol ran his New York studio, which he literally called "The Factory," like a print shop for pop culture. Using silkscreen, a process that forces ink through a stencil onto a surface, he repeated a single photograph of a soup can or a movie star dozens of times on one canvas, often shifting the color from panel to panel. The repetition itself was the comment: on advertising, on celebrity, on how many times an image can flash past us before it stops meaning anything.

EC
Elizabeth Catlett
American/Mexican, 1915–2012
THE MULTIPLE AS ACCESS

Catlett trained in printmaking partly because prints could reach far more people than a single sculpture or painting ever could. Her linocut Sharecropper (1952, printed 1970) shows a Black woman farmworker's face, dignified and weathered, carved in bold black lines. Because the block could be printed again and again, that image could travel into classrooms, homes, and movements far beyond a single gallery wall.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Silkscreen pushes ink through a stencil on fine mesh, a different process from the carved relief print you will practice this Studio, but it shares the same core idea: one master (a screen or a block) produces many identical prints. Warhol used silkscreen; Catlett and Hokusai both used relief methods (linocut and woodblock).
🔍 PART 2 · WATCH IT WORK

Because Hokusai's wave is in the public domain, we can analyze the full image in detail here. The four DAIJ steps, Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge, must go in order: describe before you analyze, analyze before you interpret, interpret before you judge.

D · Describe (just the facts)

A huge wave curls over three low boats in the foreground. Its crest breaks into many small hooked claws of foam. A snow-capped mountain sits small in the distance. The image is printed in blue, white, and pale tones from a carved woodblock.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

Hokusai builds the wave from repetition: the same clawed foam shape recurs dozens of times along the crest, creating a fast, chaotic rhythm. That rhythm is balanced against the one thing in the image that does not repeat: the still, single triangle of Mount Fuji, small and calm.

I · Interpret (what is it saying?)

The repeated foam claws make the wave feel alive and overwhelming, almost like a hundred small hands reaching for the boats, while the unmoving, unrepeated mountain suggests something permanent standing behind all that chaos. Repetition here creates the feeling of danger; the single still shape creates the feeling of endurance.

J · Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds completely, and its success depended on being a print. Because Hokusai carved this design once and printed it thousands of times, an image built entirely around the power of repetition became one of the most repeated images in art history.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH DAIJ STEP?
Read each statement about a print. Decide which DAIJ step it belongs to, then check it.
"The same triangular motif repeats in a straight line across the top border of the print."
"The pattern succeeds because its exact repetition is what makes the surface feel calm and orderly rather than random."
"There are six rows of a repeated leaf shape printed in dark green ink."
▶  In your notebook, write one Interpret sentence about Hokusai's wave before you open this. Then compare.
A strong Interpret sentence connects the technique to a feeling or idea. For example: "The repeated clawed foam makes the wave feel alive and multiplying, almost too fast to survive, while the single unrepeated mountain feels like the one thing that will still be there after the wave passes." Your wording will differ; what matters is that your interpretation is built on what you already described and analyzed.
✍️ NOW YOU TRY
Choose whichever work pulls your eye more: Andy Warhol's Campbell's Soup Cans or Marilyn Diptych, or Elizabeth Catlett's Sharecropper (both linked above). In your notebook, run the same four steps, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, on the one you picked.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Warhol repeated a soup can to comment on mass culture. Catlett repeated a portrait so it could reach more people. Hokusai repeated foam to make a wave feel alive. Three very different reasons, one shared tool: repetition is never neutral. It always says something.
