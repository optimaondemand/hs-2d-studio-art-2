STUDIO 5 · LESSON 3
Motif, Pattern & Negative Space
Design the single unit your whole print series will be built from. Keep a pencil and paper beside you. ~50 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Design a repeatable motif, arrange it into pattern with a clear rhythm, and read the positive and negative space a repeat creates. These tools turn one small shape into a surface that feels planned rather than scattered.
📚 PART 1 · THREE IDEAS THAT BUILD A PATTERN

A motif is the single shape or unit you repeat. A pattern is what appears once that motif is arranged and repeated across a surface. A rhythm is the visual beat that the repeated motif creates as your eye moves across the pattern.

MotifA Japanese katagami stencil: the physical master template a dyer cut so one motif could be pressed onto cloth again and again. This is the real ancestor of the "motif" idea.
Unknown maker, Kata-gami: Umbrellas, water and pine needle clusters, late 19th c., Cooper Hewitt, Smithsonian Design Museum. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
Regular RhythmThe same willow-leaf motif repeats at even, predictable spacing, row after row. The eye settles into a calm, steady beat.
William Morris, Willow Bough wallpaper, 1887. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
Progressive RhythmNot a printed design, but the closest honest natural example: each chamber of the shell is the same basic unit, repeated, but growing a little larger every time. The eye reads it as building outward, not just repeating.
Florian Elias Rieser, Section cut Nautilus, 2008. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.
💡 TWO KINDS OF RHYTHM, ONE MOTIF
The exact same leaf motif can create a completely different feeling depending on how you repeat it. Even, regular spacing feels orderly and calm, almost like wallpaper. Gradually changing size or spacing feels alive and directional, almost like growth. Neither is "correct"; the choice depends on what you want the pattern to say.
🔄 PART 2 · POSITIVE AND NEGATIVE SPACE IN A REPEAT

In a repeated pattern, the motif itself is positive space. But the moment you repeat it, the gaps between one motif and the next become their own shapes: the negative space of the repeat. A strong pattern designer plans both, not just the motif.

Notice that the pale stone between the carved tiles is not empty by accident. It forms its own repeating star shape, planned as intentionally as the motif itself. This particular tiling repeats in a straight grid rather than the offset, row-shifted half-drop repeat described above, but it proves the same point: if you only design the motif and ignore the gaps, the pattern often looks cluttered or uneven no matter how carefully the motif itself is drawn.

✍️ PART 3 · GUIDED PRACTICE (do this now)

No printmaking tools yet, just pencil and paper. You are designing the motif itself, the same design thinking Hokusai's workshop did before any wood was ever carved.

  1. Sketch three motif options (8 min). Base each on a simple natural form: a leaf, a shell spiral, a feather barb, a wave curl. Keep each one small, about the size of a thumbnail, and simple enough to repeat easily.
  2. Pick one and repeat it by hand (10 min). On a fresh section of paper, draw your chosen motif six to nine times in a grid. Try to keep the spacing even.
  3. Check the negative space (5 min). Look only at the gaps between your repeated motifs. Are they even, or do some crowd together while others feel too empty? Adjust your spacing until the gaps feel as intentional as the motif itself.
🏷️ A GOOD PRINT MOTIF SHARES THREE TRAITS
It is simple enough to carve (avoid tiny thin details that are hard to cut cleanly), it has a clear silhouette that still reads when repeated small, and it tiles without awkward gaps when placed next to copies of itself. Natural forms like leaves, shells, and feathers tend to satisfy all three.

📝 VOCABULARY
MotifThe single shape or unit that gets repeated to build a pattern.
PatternA design built from a repeated motif arranged across a surface.
RhythmThe visual beat created as the eye moves across a repeated motif.
Regular rhythmRepetition at even, predictable spacing.
Progressive rhythmRepetition that gradually changes in size, spacing, or form.
Negative space (in a repeat)The shapes formed by the gaps between repeated motifs.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

A motif is only as strong as the pattern it can build. Before you touch a carving tool, the real design work happens here: choosing a simple, clear shape and deciding how it will repeat, in a steady beat or a shifting one, with gaps as intentional as the shape itself.
