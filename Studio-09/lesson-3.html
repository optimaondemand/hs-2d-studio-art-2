STUDIO 9 · LESSON 3
The Abstraction Ladder
Four named stages between a careful study and a shape with no subject at all. ~50 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Name and recognize the four stages of the abstraction ladder: realistic, simplified, stylized, and non-representational. Take a single observed motif through all four stages yourself, keeping control of the decisions at every step.
📚 PART 1 · ONE LEAF, FOUR STAGES

Below is a single maple leaf, taken through the same four stages every abstraction journey in this Studio will follow. Read left to right. Notice that each stage removes or changes something specific from the one before it. Nothing here is random.

STAGE 1RealisticEvery vein, lobe, and edge irregularity observed and recorded as accurately as possible. This is the direct-observation drawing, the same skill from Studio 1.
Becca, Maple Leaf Macro. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0.
STAGE 2SimplifiedLook at the plain outline leaf near the bottom right of this scientific plate (labeled 9): the lobed silhouette and main veins are kept, but color and surface texture are gone. The leaf is still unmistakably a leaf, but extra detail is stripped away.
Philipp Franz von Siebold & Joseph Gerhard Zuccarini, Flora Japonica, Tab. 145, 1870. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
STAGE 3StylizedThis centuries-old Japanese family crest gives the maple leaf a bold, invented visual language: one flat color, a clean symmetrical shape, no vein detail at all. A designer's version, not a botanist's.
Sumikiri-kaku ni hitoha momiji, a traditional Japanese maple-leaf family crest (kamon). Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
STAGE 4Non-representationalDove, working in the same period as O'Keeffe, pushed natural forms until only shape, color, and rhythm remained. Without a caption, nothing here points back to any single leaf or plant.
Arthur Dove, Nature Symbolized, 1911. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
💡 WHY THE LADDER HAS RUNGS, NOT A CLIFF
These four real images come from four different makers and moments, a photographer, an 1870 science plate, a Japanese family crest, and a 1911 painting, chosen because each one is the clearest real example of its stage. No single stage jumps straight from a botanical drawing to pure shape. Each stage changes one or two things and keeps the rest. That is what makes the journey traceable. A viewer (or a grader) should be able to look at your four stages and see exactly which decision happened at each step.
✍️ PART 2 · GUIDED PRACTICE (do this now)

Pick one small object with a simple, clear silhouette (an apple, a leaf, a mug, a single flower). Work through all four stages on one page, left to right, exactly like the diagram above.

  1. Realistic (5 min). Draw the object from direct observation. Record its true outline, proportions, and any distinctive detail. This is your anchor; everything after this is a deliberate change from it.
  2. Simplified (4 min). Redraw it using only its major shapes. Remove small details, but keep the overall proportions and silhouette recognizable.
  3. Stylized (4 min). Redraw it again, but this time exaggerate one quality on purpose, a curve, a color choice, a line weight, and apply that same choice consistently across the whole shape.
  4. Non-representational (4 min). Take just the shapes and colors from your stylized version and rearrange or isolate them so the connection to the original object is no longer obvious on its own.
⚡ TRY IT · NAME THE STAGE
Read each description. Decide which ladder stage it describes, then check it.
A drawing of a bird where every feather is gone, replaced by one bold, curved shape and a single flat color used consistently.
Three overlapping colored shapes on a page. Without the earlier stages beside it, nothing here points to what it came from.
A drawing of a hand where the fingers are shown as smooth, rounded shapes, with no wrinkles or nails, but the proportions still match a real hand.
🖉️ SKILL BUILDER SKETCH (turn in this week)

The Four-Stage Object

On one sheet, take a single simple object through all four stages of the abstraction ladder: realistic, simplified, stylized, non-representational. Label each stage. This is a completion-graded practice rung, not a finished artwork. Focus on making each step a clear, deliberate move from the one before it.

Full directions and submission steps are in the Canvas assignment Skill Builder 1: The Abstraction Ladder. Photograph your page and upload it, then save a copy to your OneDrive portfolio folder.

📝 VOCABULARY
RealisticRendered as close to accurate observation as possible.
SimplifiedReduced to major shapes; true proportion kept, extra detail removed.
StylizedGiven a consistent, invented visual language rather than a literal one.
Non-representationalNo obvious connection remains to a real object or scene.
EssenceThe core visual idea that keeps a simplified form recognizable.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

The abstraction ladder is not a ranking from bad to good. It is a map. Knowing the four stages by name means you can choose exactly how far to travel, and you can always point back to the real object your journey started from.
