STUDIO 9 · LESSON 2
Georgia O'Keeffe: A Career of Looking Closer
One artist's whole body of work, read as a single long journey toward abstraction. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How far can an artist push a real subject toward abstraction and still call the result observation?
In Lesson 1 you saw one O'Keeffe painting that simplifies a real tree. Now trace her career across decades to see how the same instinct grew into her most famous work, and practice the four-step DAIJ method you learned in Studio 1 on a new kind of question: not just what is here, but how far it has been pushed from reality.
📚 PART 1 · FROM STILL LIFE TO MAGNIFICATION
GO
Georgia O'Keeffe
American, 1887–1986
THE OBSERVER WHO MOVED CLOSER

O'Keeffe trained in traditional drawing and painting, and her early career included representational still lifes and landscapes built on careful observation, the same skills you practiced in Studio 1. Over the 1920s and beyond, she developed a signature approach: take a natural form she had studied closely, often a flower, a leaf, or a bone, and paint it at a much larger scale, simplified into broad shapes of color. She insisted she was painting exactly what she saw, just asking viewers to look the way she did: slowly, up close, without the rest of the room to distract them.

By the time she moved to New Mexico, her subjects expanded to bones, shells, and desert landscapes, but the method stayed the same: observe a real form with total attention, then simplify and magnify it until the painting forces the viewer to see what she saw.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
O'Keeffe is often described as painting "magnified flowers." It is more accurate to say she magnified and simplified a form she had studied at close range for a long time. The scale and the simplification are the abstraction. The subject underneath never stopped being something she had actually observed.
🔍 PART 2 · WHAT WORKS? A DAIJ WALKTHROUGH

Return to Autumn Trees, The Maple from Lesson 1. This time, walk through it using DAIJ: Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge. You may not skip ahead. Here is the walkthrough modeled for you.

D · Describe (just the facts)

Rounded, overlapping masses of warm red, orange, and yellow fill most of the canvas. The shapes suggest clusters of leaves, but no single leaf is drawn in detail. The background is a soft, muted field.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

O'Keeffe relies on simplification of shape and a tight, warm color harmony. Individual leaf detail has been removed; what remains is the color and the rounded, clustered silhouette that our eye already associates with autumn foliage. That is enough information for the essence of the subject to survive.

I · Interpret (what is it saying?)

The painting argues that a feeling (the warmth and fullness of autumn) can be communicated more directly through simplified color and shape than through botanical accuracy. Removing detail does not remove meaning here; it concentrates it.

J · Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds because the simplification never goes so far that the subject disappears. A viewer instantly reads "autumn trees," then spends time in the color and shape relationships instead of counting leaves. That balance, recognizable but simplified, is the exact target of this Studio's project.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH DAIJ STEP?
Read each statement about O'Keeffe's work. Decide which DAIJ step it belongs to, then check it.
"The individual leaves have been simplified into a small number of large, rounded shapes."
"The painting succeeds because it stays just recognizable enough while concentrating attention on color and mood."
"Warm reds, oranges, and yellows fill most of the canvas in rounded, overlapping shapes."
▶  In your notebook, write one Interpret sentence about Autumn Trees, The Maple before you open this. Then compare.
A strong Interpret sentence connects the visible technique to a meaning without yet passing judgment. For example: "By simplifying the leaves into broad color shapes, O'Keeffe makes the painting feel like the warmth of autumn rather than a record of any one tree." Your wording will differ; what matters is that it reads meaning out of what you already described and analyzed.

📝 VOCABULARY
EssenceThe core visual idea that makes a simplified form still recognizable as its subject.
ExaggerationPushing one quality of a subject, such as scale or a curve, further than reality shows it.
MagnificationEnlarging a small observed form so the viewer must look at it differently.
CroppingChoosing what falls inside the frame, and what is cut away.
DAIJThe four-step critique order: Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

O'Keeffe's whole career can be read as one long argument: looking closely and simplifying are not opposites. The closer she looked, the more she found worth keeping, and the more she felt free to leave out. DAIJ is how you learn to see that same argument at work in any piece, including your own.
