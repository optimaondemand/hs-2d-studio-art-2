STUDIO 9 · LESSON 6
Refine, Reflect & Present
A transformation is only convincing if the viewer can trace it. Critique your progression, refine it, and present it well. ~55 min + studio time
🔄 PART 1 · LINE UP YOUR STAGES

Lay out your anchor observational drawing, your transitional stages, and your final stylized or abstract piece in order, left to right. This is the progression a viewer, and your teacher, will read.

👀 LOOK AT THE WHOLE SEQUENCE
Can you point to exactly what changed between each stage, and why? If a stage feels like it happened by accident rather than by decision, that is the one to refine now. A strong sequence reads like a clear argument: this is where I started, this is what I chose to change, and here is where that choice led.
🔍 PART 2 · CRITIQUE YOUR OWN PROGRESSION

Before you call it done, critique your own sequence using the same four-step order you used on O'Keeffe in Lesson 2: describe, analyze, interpret, judge. Be your own first critic.

D · Describe

Lay the stages side by side. What does each one actually show? Is the anchor drawing genuinely observational, or did you rush it?

A · Analyze

Which abstraction tools did you use at each stage: simplification, exaggeration, stylization, cropping, magnification? Can you name at least two, and point to exactly where each one happened?

I · Interpret

Does your final piece still carry the essence of the original subject, even after everything you changed? What would a viewer with no context still recognize, or feel, about it?

J · Judge

What one change would most improve the sequence? Usually it is redrawing a weak middle stage or pushing the final piece further so the transformation feels intentional rather than timid.

💭 REVISION CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
You look at your final piece and it barely differs from your anchor drawing. What is the most likely fix?
📝 PART 3 · WRITE YOUR ARTIST STATEMENT

Your artist statement travels with the artwork. It is two or three focused sentences, in your own voice, that show your transformation was a series of decisions. Type it into the submission text box with your drawings.

A strong statement answers three things:

  • What object or natural form did you choose, and why?
  • Which abstraction moves did you use on purpose, and at which stage? (Name at least two: simplification, exaggeration, stylization, cropping, magnification.)
  • What surprised you about how far the subject could travel and still feel connected to where it started?
▶  Draft your three sentences in your notebook first, then open this for a sample of the register (do not copy it).
"I chose a single maple leaf because its vein pattern gave me something clear to simplify. In my second stage I reduced the lobed outline to one smooth curve, then in my final piece I cropped in tightly on that curve and repeated it three times in a warm color palette, borrowed from how O'Keeffe uses color to carry a subject's feeling. I was surprised that the final piece still felt like autumn even though no single shape in it looks like a leaf anymore." Your statement should be this specific, but about your own subject and your own choices.

Sign your final piece. Every stage in your progression is evidence of your thinking, but the signature marks the finished statement: this is mine, I made these decisions, I stand behind them.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

You began Studio 9 with one accurate drawing of one real object. Two weeks of deliberate decisions carried it somewhere new, without ever cutting the thread back to where it started. That is the discipline behind almost every stylized and abstract artwork you will ever study: not invention out of nothing, but a journey with a beginning you can always point back to.
