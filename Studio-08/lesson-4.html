STUDIO 8 · LESSON 4
Curation, Sequencing & Capturing Your Work Well
Choosing which pieces to show, what order to show them in, and how to photograph them so the choices are visible. ~50 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Select four to six pieces from Studios 1–7 for your portfolio, arrange them in a sequence that tells a clear story of growth, and photograph or scan each one so a viewer can actually see what you made.
📚 PART 1 · ORDER IS A DECISION, NOT AN ACCIDENT

Two curators could select the exact same six pieces and still build two very different portfolios, just by changing the order. Chronological order (Studio 1 first, Studio 7 last) tells one story: growth over time. Grouping by theme or technique tells another: range and versatility. Ending on your strongest piece tells a third: this is what I can do. Every order says something, whether you plan it or not.

The sequence above ends on strength on purpose. A viewer's last impression tends to stick, so most curators close a set with one of its best pieces rather than its weakest, even if the weakest piece came last chronologically.

💬 WONDER FIRST
If you sequenced your six chosen pieces purely by date made, would the story it tells be the one you actually want to tell? Try laying your shortlist out in date order, then try it again ending on your strongest piece. Notice what changes.
⚖️ PART 2 · CURATION MEANS LEAVING SOME OUT

You have seven Studio pieces and only need four to six, which means leaving one to three out entirely. Leaving a piece out is not a punishment; it is what curation is. A crowded portfolio with every piece included, strong and weak alike, is harder to read than a tight one that makes a clear point.

  1. Sort into three piles. Definitely include, definitely leave out, and undecided. Most students find this fast for the first two piles.
  2. Break ties with your Lesson 2 pattern. If two pieces are close, keep the one that best shows the strength or the honest growth-in-progress you named in your self-critique.
  3. Include at least one revised piece. Your Lesson 3 revision plan should show up somewhere in this set; it is proof you can improve, not just produce.
  4. Land on four to six. Fewer than four does not show enough range; more than six starts to dilute your strongest choices.
⚡ TRY IT · CURATION OR JUST INCLUDING EVERYTHING?
Read each approach to building a mid-year set. Decide whether it is genuine curation, then check it.
"I am including five of my seven pieces. I left out my weakest observation drawing and a collage that repeats the same idea as a stronger piece I kept."
"I am submitting all seven pieces so the teacher can see everything I made this semester."
📷 PART 3 · A BLURRY PHOTO UNDOES A STRONG PIECE

A viewer can only judge what the photo shows them. A well-made piece captured under a yellow lamp, at an angle, with a shadow across half of it, reads as weaker than it actually is. Capturing your own work well is a real skill and a real part of curation, not an afterthought.

AVOID

Shot at an angle under mixed indoor lighting, one corner in shadow, glare across the surface, the piece filling only part of the frame.

AIM FOR

Shot straight-on in even daylight or diffuse light, no glare or shadow, the piece filling the frame with a clean, uncluttered background.

Straight-onCamera held parallel to the flat artwork, not tilted, so edges stay rectangular and nothing distorts.
Even lightDiffuse daylight or bounced light with no single strong lamp casting a hot spot or a hard shadow.
Fill the frameThe artwork takes up most of the photo, with a plain, uncluttered background around the edges.
ResolutionEnough image detail that texture and fine value changes are still visible when viewed at full size.
▶  Before you open this, photograph one of your own pieces right now and check it against the four capture terms above.
Most first attempts fail on lighting before anything else: a lamp or a window creates a hot glare or a long shadow across part of the piece. Move near a window with indirect daylight, hold the camera flat and parallel to the work, and retake the shot. Compare the two; the difference is usually immediate.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Curation is choosing what to leave out. Sequencing is choosing what story the choices tell. Capture is making sure a viewer can actually see the choices you made. All three are skills, and all three are gradable, teachable parts of being an artist.
