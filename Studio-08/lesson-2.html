STUDIO 8 · LESSON 2
Self-Critique Across Your Work
You already know the critique method. Today you turn it on yourself, across seven pieces at once. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
Can you critique your own work as honestly as you critique someone else's?
In Studio 1 you learned a four-step critique method, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, to read a master's drawing, then turned it on one piece of your own. Today you run it across your whole body of work: seven pieces, one method, looking for patterns you cannot see when you only look at one piece at a time.
📚 PART 1 · ONE PIECE VS. SEVEN PIECES

Critiquing one drawing tells you about that drawing. Critiquing seven drawings made over four months tells you about you: which skills you reach for automatically, which ones you avoid, and where the same weakness keeps showing up under a different subject. A pattern that appears once might be an accident; a pattern that appears three times is a habit, and habits are exactly what revision should target.

Studio 1 · value is flat
Studio 6 · value is flat again

Notice something in the pair above: two very different Studios (observation drawing, then a mixed-media collage) share the same weak spot, thin value range. Seen alone, each piece might read as "pretty good." Seen together, a clear target for revision appears. That is the power of reviewing a body of work instead of a single piece.

🔍 PART 2 · RUN THE CRITIQUE ACROSS ALL SEVEN

Same four steps you already know. The only change is scope: instead of describing one piece, you describe the set, with your own Studios 1 through 7 open in front of you.

D · Describe (just the facts, across all seven)

Lay out your seven pieces (physically or as photos on one screen). What media did you use most? Which Studios are drawing, which are painted, collaged, or mixed? Just list what is there, no opinions yet.

A · Analyze (how is the set built?)

Which elements or techniques show up strong in more than one piece (a confident line, a bold color choice, a strong composition)? Which ones show up weak in more than one piece? Look specifically for repeats, not one-time issues.

I · Interpret (what does the set say about you as an artist?)

What kind of subject matter keeps appearing? What mood or approach shows up more than once? What do these seven pieces, together, say you care about or are drawn to?

J · Judge (what is the one thing worth fixing?)

Of everything you noticed in Analyze, which single weakness would most improve your work if you fixed it in one or two pieces right now? That answer becomes your revision plan in Lesson 3.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH CRITIQUE STEP?
Read each statement about a body of work. Decide which critique step it belongs to, then check it.
"My value range is strong in Studio 4 and Studio 7, but flat in Studio 1, Studio 3, and Studio 6."
"The one change that would help my portfolio most is pushing value darker in my two weakest pieces before I submit."
"Across all seven Studios, I keep choosing quiet, close-up subjects. I think I am drawn to intimacy over drama."
▶  In your notebook, write your own Describe, Analyze, Interpret, and Judge for your seven Studios before you open this.
There is no single correct answer here, this is about your own work, but a strong Judge sentence always names one specific, doable action ("I will darken the shadows in Studio 3" rather than "I should get better at value"). If your Judge step is vague, go back to Analyze and find a more specific pattern to point at.
📝 VOCABULARY
Self-critiqueApplying the critique method honestly to your own work, not just someone else's.
PatternA strength or weakness that repeats across more than one piece, rather than appearing once.
RevisionA genuine improvement to a finished piece, guided by specific critique.
HabitA tendency, good or bad, that shows up automatically across an artist's work.
➡️ AFTER THIS LESSON
1
Complete: Portfolio Review Check-In · Week 1

💪 THE BIG IDEA

One critique tells you about one piece. Seven critiques, read together, tell you about yourself. The critique method scales from a single artwork to a whole body of work without changing its steps, only its scope.
