STUDIO 8 · LESSON 3
Choosing What to Revise
Not every piece needs revision. Learn to pick well, then plan the change. ~45 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Choose one or two pieces from Studios 1–7 worth revising, tell the difference between a genuine revision and a wasteful redo, and write a specific plan naming exactly what will change and why.
📚 PART 1 · A REVISION IS NOT A REDO

It is tempting, after critiquing your own work, to throw a piece out and start completely over. Resist that: starting over teaches you almost nothing about revision, it just repeats the same process. A true revision keeps the piece and makes a targeted, deliberate change guided by exactly what your critique found.

BEFORE · ORIGINAL PIECE

Composition and subject are already working. Value stays in a narrow middle range: no true darks, no true lights. The form reads as flat from across the room.

→

AFTER · REVISED PIECE

Same composition, same subject, kept exactly as it was. The artist pushed the darkest shadows much darker and protected the brightest highlights. Nothing else changed. The form now reads as solid.

⚖️ REVISION vs. REDO
NOT A REVISION

Erasing the whole piece and starting a new drawing of the same subject from scratch. This avoids the hard part: improving what already exists.

A REAL REVISION

Keeping the composition and subject, then making one or two targeted changes (deepen value, fix one proportion, sharpen a focal point) guided by specific critique.

✍️ PART 2 · GUIDED PRACTICE (do this now)

Use your Lesson 2 Judge step to choose your revision target. Work through these steps to turn it into a real plan.

  1. Pick one or two pieces (not more). Choose from Studios 1–7 the piece, or two pieces, where your identified weakness is clearest and where a targeted fix is realistic in the time you have.
  2. Name the exact change. Write one sentence per piece: "In [Studio], I will [specific change] to fix [specific weakness]." Avoid vague goals like "make it better."
  3. Predict the result. In one sentence, say what the piece will do differently once revised (read as more solid, hold the eye longer, feel more intentional).
  4. Set a stopping point. Decide in advance how much time this revision gets. A revision that never ends is a redo in disguise.
⚡ TRY IT · REVISION OR REDO?
Read each plan. Decide whether it is a genuine revision or actually a redo, then check it.
"I will keep my Studio 3 color piece exactly as composed, but repaint the shadow areas with a cooler, darker blue to create stronger contrast."
"My Studio 5 print did not turn out how I wanted, so I am going to carve a brand new block with a different design."
🖉️ PROCESS CHECK (turn in this week)

Revision Plan

Write a short revision plan naming the one or two pieces you will revise, the exact change you will make to each, and why (tie it back to your Lesson 2 Judge step). This is a completion-graded planning document, not the revised artwork itself.

Full directions and submission steps are in the Canvas assignment Portfolio Review Check-In. You will act on this plan starting in Lesson 5.

📝 VOCABULARY
RevisionA targeted, deliberate improvement to an existing piece, keeping most of it intact.
RedoStarting over completely rather than improving what already exists.
Targeted changeOne specific, named fix rather than a vague intention to "make it better."
Revision planA short written statement of exactly what will change in a piece, and why.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Revision is surgical, not a restart. The best revisions come from a specific critique finding, name one exact change, and know when to stop.
