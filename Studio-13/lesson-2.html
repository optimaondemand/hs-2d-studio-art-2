STUDIO 13 · LESSON 2
Three Routes to the Unconscious
Three artists, three very different ways into the same dream logic. Then DAIJ, applied to a Studio 13 work. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
If three artists all painted "the unconscious," why do their paintings look nothing alike?
Surrealism was never one style. It was a shared goal, painting what the rule-following mind edits out, approached from three different directions. Meet the three artists behind this Studio's featured works, then use DAIJ to read one of them closely.
📚 PART 1 · THREE ROUTES, ONE GOAL

All three works below are still under copyright, so you will meet them through description and a verified museum link, not through an image reproduced on this page. Read each description slowly before you click through.

SD
Salvador Dalí
Spanish, 1904–1989
HYPER-REAL DISLOCATION

Dalí called his method "hand-painted dream photographs." He rendered every object with sharp, photographic clarity, then placed those objects in relationships that broke physical law. His most famous painting shows pocket watches gone soft, draped over rocks and a bare branch like wet cloth, under a strange, still coastal light. Painted loosely, it would look like a cartoon; painted with total control, it looks like a fact you cannot explain.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the museum site)
Salvador Dalí, The Persistence of Memory, 1931

Look for exactly one broken rule per object: rigid metal turned soft, a static object placed on a bare, dreamlike shore. Notice how ordinary the light and horizon look by comparison.

Salvador Dalí, The Persistence of Memory, 1931, at the Museum of Modern Art
RM
René Magritte
Belgian, 1898–1967
THE QUIETLY WRONG

Where Dalí painted the spectacular, Magritte painted the deceptively calm. His scenes often look, for a second, like an ordinary room or sky, until you notice the one relationship that cannot be true: ordinary objects swollen to enormous scale and pressed into a small interior, or crammed against a window where they do not belong. Magritte's dislocation is scale and placement, not distortion of the object itself. The comb is still a comb; the room is still a room; it is only their relationship to each other that has gone wrong.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the museum site)
René Magritte, Les valeurs personnelles (Personal Values), 1952

Look for objects at an impossible scale inside a small bedroom, and notice that nothing about the objects themselves is distorted. The wrongness is entirely in the relationship between their size and the room's.

René Magritte, Les valeurs personnelles, 1952, at SFMOMA
RV
Remedios Varo
Spanish-Mexican, 1908–1963
TRANSFORMATION & MYTH

Varo, who worked for most of her career in Mexico City, painted figures caught mid-transformation: bodies fusing with architecture, animals, and invented machines, often set inside her own invented mythology. Her surrealism leans hardest on metamorphosis, one form becoming another within a single image, and on symbolic, dreamlike narrative rather than a single startling object. Her paintings reward slow looking: each invented detail (a strange instrument, a figure's fused limb) is another piece of a private myth.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the museum site)
Remedios Varo, Dolor remático, 1948, and Paraíso de los gatos, 1955

Look for the metamorphosis: where does a body stop and an object or animal begin? Notice how Varo treats the transformation as ordinary within the painting's own dream logic, not as a shock.

Remedios Varo works at the Museo de Arte Moderno, Mexico City
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
All three artists were part of the same movement but were not painting the same painting. Dalí distorted the object's material. Magritte distorted its scale and placement while leaving the object itself alone. Varo fused one form into another. When you plan your own Studio Project, you are choosing which of these devices, or which combination, will carry your idea.
🔍 PART 2 · WATCH IT WORK

DAIJ reads a work in order: Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge. No skipping ahead. Here is DAIJ applied to The Persistence of Memory.

D · Describe (just the facts)

Several soft, drooping pocket watches rest on a rocky ledge, a bare branch, and what may be a sleeping face. A calm, flat shoreline and pale sky stretch behind them. The light is even and clear, almost like early morning.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

Dalí uses hyper-precise rendering and even, calm lighting to make the scene feel documented rather than imagined. The single broken rule, rigid metal turned soft, is isolated against an otherwise stable, believable landscape, which makes that one impossibility stand out sharply.

I · Interpret (what is it saying?)

Time itself has gone soft and unreliable, perhaps mirroring how memory does not keep time the way clocks do. The painting suggests that inside the mind, on the far side of sleep, the rules that govern the outer world simply do not hold.

J · Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds because the technical control never wavers even as the subject becomes impossible. That contrast, perfect craft applied to an idea that could not be photographed, is the entire engine of the painting's unease.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH DAIJ STEP?
Read each statement about a surreal artwork. Decide which DAIJ step it belongs to, then check it.
"The oversized comb is rendered with the same careful shading as the small, ordinary bed beside it, which makes the size difference read as fact rather than error."
"A large wooden comb, a matchstick, and a bar of soap fill most of a small bedroom."
"The painting succeeds because the calm, ordinary bedroom makes the oversized objects feel more unsettling than a wilder, more chaotic scene would."
▶  In your notebook, write one Interpret sentence about Varo's transformations before you open this. Then compare.
A strong Interpret sentence goes beyond what is shown to what it might mean. For example: "Varo's fused figures suggest that identity itself is not fixed, that a person and their tools, or a person and the natural world, might be more continuous than we usually assume." Your wording will differ; what matters is that your interpretation is grounded in details you actually described and analyzed first, not invented from nothing.
✍️ NOW YOU TRY
Choose whichever work pulls your eye more: Dalí's The Persistence of Memory above, Magritte's Les valeurs personnelles, or Varo's Dolor remático or Paraíso de los gatos at the museum links. In your notebook, run the same four steps, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, on the one you picked.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Dalí melted materials. Magritte broke scale. Varo fused forms into myth. Three different devices, aimed at the same target: making an inner truth visible with outer precision. DAIJ is how you read any of their choices with care instead of a shrug.
