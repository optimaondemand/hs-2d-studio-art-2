STUDIO 14 · LESSON 3
Message & Audience
Your first hands-on tools for making an image argue something on purpose. ~50 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Combine an image and text so each one changes how the viewer reads the other, name a specific audience for a message, and compress an argument into a short, memorable slogan. These three moves are the engine behind every poster in this Studio's featured artists' careers.
📚 PART 1 · IMAGE + TEXT, NOT IMAGE THEN TEXT

A caption describes an image. Image-text is different: the words and the picture are built together so neither one means the same thing alone. Change the text and the image reads differently. Change the image and the same words hit differently.

The three references below are real: a real documentary photograph, the same photograph with its real historical caption, and a real 1917 recruitment poster. They are shown only to demonstrate the form, image alone, then caption, then true image-text, not to endorse any message.

Image aloneShows a subject. The viewer supplies their own reading, which can be anything at all.
Dorothea Lange, Migrant Mother, Nipomo, California, 1936. Farm Security Administration, public domain (US government work). Wikimedia Commons.
CaptionDescribes the image after the fact. Explains, but does not argue. This is the photograph's real Library of Congress caption: "Destitute pea pickers in California. Mother of seven children. Age thirty-two."
Dorothea Lange, Migrant Mother, Nipomo, California, 1936. Farm Security Administration, public domain (US government work). Wikimedia Commons.
Image-textBuilt as one unit. The words reframe the image into an argument the image alone could not make.
James Montgomery Flagg, I Want You for U.S. Army, 1917. Public domain. Wikimedia Commons.
💡 A QUICK TEST
If you could remove the text and the image would still make the same point on its own, the text is a caption. If removing the text collapses the argument, you have real image-text. Aim for the second one.
🎯 PART 2 · NAME YOUR AUDIENCE BEFORE YOU DESIGN

An audience is the specific group of people a work is built to reach and move. "Everyone" is not an audience; it is the absence of one. The clearer your audience, the sharper your image and words can be, because you know exactly what that group already believes and what would actually change their mind.

Classmateswho walk past a hallway poster daily
Adultswho make a specific decision (voters, shoppers, parents)
People who agreeand need a rallying image
People who disagreeand need a reason to reconsider

Notice that the last two audiences need different art. A poster for people who already agree can use insider shorthand and celebrate a shared identity. A poster meant to reach people who disagree usually needs to lead with a fact or an image they cannot dismiss, before it asks for anything.

✍️ PART 3 · SLOGAN ECONOMY

A slogan is a short, memorable phrase built to be repeated and to stick. "Slogan economy" means every word has to earn its place. A viewer gives a poster about one second; a sentence they cannot finish in that second is wasted.

"We believe that everyone in our community deserves access to clean drinking water regardless of their income level or zip code."
CLEAN WATER ISN'T A LUXURY.

Same argument, radically fewer words. The short version keeps the claim and drops the explanation; the image is left to do some of that explaining instead.

  1. Write the long version first. Say your full argument as a sentence or two, no editing yet. This is your idea, not your slogan.
  2. Cut it in half, twice. Each pass, remove the least essential word or phrase. Keep the verb and the claim; cut qualifiers and explanations.
  3. Read it aloud in under two seconds. If it takes longer, cut again. A slogan you cannot say quickly will not be read quickly either.
⚡ TRY IT · MESSAGE, AUDIENCE, OR SLOGAN?

Before sketching anything, a designer asks: "Am I talking to people who already care about this, or people who have never thought about it?"
A designer rewrites "we need to reduce the amount of plastic waste our school produces every single day" down to "LESS PLASTIC. LESS WASTE."

💪 THE BIG IDEA

A message becomes persuasive when image and text depend on each other, when it is built for a specific audience instead of everyone, and when it is cut down to what a viewer can actually read in a glance. None of that requires talent alone; it requires deliberate editing.
