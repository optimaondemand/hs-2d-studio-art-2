STUDIO 14 · LESSON 4
Responsible Commentary
The craft of arguing fairly, plus a fact you need before you start editing images. ~50 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Separate fact from opinion in your own claims, check a message for fairness and respect before you finish it, and recognize why a manipulated image is a different kind of problem than a slogan you disagree with.
📚 PART 1 · FACT VS. OPINION

Every cause-based artwork mixes two kinds of claims. A fact is something you can check and verify. An opinion is a judgment or a value, something reasonable people can disagree about even after they agree on the facts. Strong commentary keeps these straight and never disguises one as the other.

FACT (checkable)"Our school cafeteria throws away food every day at lunch."
OPINION (a judgment)"Throwing away that much food is wrong and we should be ashamed of it."

Both sentences can appear in the same artwork. The problem is not having an opinion; every social-commentary artwork has one. The problem is stating an opinion with the confidence of a fact, or inventing a "fact" to make your opinion look stronger than the evidence supports.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
If your poster makes a factual claim (a number, a statistic, "most people..."), you should be able to name where that claim came from. If you cannot, soften it into an opinion or cut it. A poster with one honest fact is stronger than one with three claims you cannot back up.
🤝 PART 2 · ARGUE THE IDEA, NOT THE PERSON

Look back at the propaganda-vs-persuasion comparison from Lesson 1: responsible persuasion argues against an idea or a policy, never against a group of people. That distinction becomes a practical checklist once you are actually designing.

⚠️ RISKS TO WATCH FOR

  • Mocking or stereotyping the people on one side of an issue
  • Naming or picturing a real classmate, teacher, or private individual without their consent
  • Using shock or fear as the whole argument, with no honest claim underneath it
  • Exaggerating a statistic because the true number "wasn't dramatic enough"

✅ A FAIRER APPROACH

  • Target the policy, habit, or system, not a group of people
  • Use general figures or your own composed imagery, not real people's likenesses
  • Let one honest, checkable claim carry the emotional weight
  • Ask: would someone who disagrees with me say this is a fair description of their view?
▶  In your notebook, name one issue and write one sentence that argues the idea, not a person. Then open this.
A useful test: read your sentence back and ask whether it names a policy, habit, or system ("the dress code," "food waste," "screen time before bed") rather than a person or a group ("kids who," "people who always"). If your sentence would still make sense with the specific group swapped out for "this policy," you are arguing the idea.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND MANIPULATED IMAGES
AI image tools can now generate or alter a photograph so convincingly that most viewers cannot tell it was changed: a crowd made to look bigger or smaller, a quote attached to a face that never said it, an event that never happened rendered as if a camera had caught it. This is a different problem than an opinion you disagree with. An opinion is visible as an opinion; a manipulated image can pass as unedited evidence. For your Studio Project, use only images you photographed yourself, drew yourself, or that you can identify and credit honestly. If you use AI at any stage, say so plainly in your artist statement. Presenting an AI-altered image as a real, unedited photograph is not covered by any of the tools you learned in this Studio; it is a different kind of dishonesty, and it undermines the very trust a responsible message depends on.
⚡ TRY IT · FACT, OPINION, OR HARM RISK?
Read each statement a student is considering putting on a poster. Sort it correctly, then check.
"Our district cut art class time by 30 minutes a week this year."
"Cutting art class shows the district does not value creativity."
A poster puts a real photo of the principal's face next to the words "DOESN'T CARE ABOUT US."
🖉️ SKILL BUILDER SKETCH (turn in this week)

Fact/Opinion Message Audit

Take the slogan you drafted in Lesson 3 (or write a new one for a cause you are considering). List every claim it makes, factual or implied, and label each one Fact or Opinion. For each Fact, note where the information came from. For each Opinion, confirm it argues the idea or policy, not a person or group. This is a completion-graded practice rung, not a finished artwork.

Full directions and submission steps are in the Canvas assignment Skill Builder 2: Fact/Opinion Audit. Upload your audit as a photo or typed document, and save a copy to your OneDrive portfolio folder.

EthicsPrinciples about what is fair and right to do, applied here to how art argues.
