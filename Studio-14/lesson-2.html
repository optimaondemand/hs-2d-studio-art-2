STUDIO 14 · LESSON 2
Kruger & Fairey
Two artists, two very different careers, one shared toolkit of image and text. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How do two artists, working decades apart, use the exact same tools to say completely different things?
In Lesson 1 you met the tools of social commentary art in the abstract. Now watch two working artists actually use them, and practice the four-step method critics use to read any work of art.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Barbara Kruger has spent decades making work that looks almost the same every time: black-and-white found photography, cropped tight, with declarative red-and-white text laid across it in a bold sans-serif type. The visual below is an original concept graphic built to show that formula, not a reproduction of any one Kruger piece.

Kruger's actual work, made since the 1980s, uses this formula to interrogate power, consumerism, and identity, often with pronouns ("you," "your," "I") that put the viewer directly inside the sentence. Her work is under copyright, so view it at its museum source rather than here.

📚 PART 2 · TWO ARTISTS, TWO TOOLKITS

Social commentary art is not one style. Compare a gallery artist known for text-driven critique with a street artist known for image-first icons that spread through wheat-pasted posters and stickers before they ever entered a museum.

BK
Barbara Kruger
American, b. 1945, living
THE TEXT-FIRST CRITIC

Kruger worked for years as a magazine designer before turning that skill on advertising itself. Her signature move is appropriation: taking the visual language of advertising, found photographs, bold type, direct address, and turning it back on the viewer to question consumerism, power, and gender. The text usually does the heaviest lifting; the image sets the trap.

SF
Shepard Fairey
American, b. 1970, living
THE IMAGE-FIRST STREET ARTIST

Fairey started with stickers and stencils spread through public space, an intentionally unauthorized, low-cost distribution method. His posters combine a strong, often stylized portrait image with a short slogan in bold type. Where Kruger leads with words, Fairey usually leads with a striking image and lets a short phrase seal the message.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the artist/museum site)
Shepard Fairey, Rise Above and Glass Houses, 2010

Bold, high-contrast portrait-style posters built for public walls: a strong central image, a limited color palette, and a short phrase that names the work's argument in three words or fewer.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Appropriation means borrowing an existing image, style, or format and reusing it to make a new point. Both artists appropriate: Kruger borrows the look of advertising, Fairey borrows the look of propaganda posters and street stencils. Appropriation is a legitimate artistic tool when it transforms the source into a new statement, not when it simply copies someone else's original work as your own.
🔍 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

Artists and critics read a work in a deliberate order so judgment is earned, not blurted: Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge. You may not skip ahead. Here is the critique process applied to Kruger's Untitled (I shop therefore I am).

D · Describe (just the facts)

A black-and-white photograph of a hand holding a small card. Bold red-and-white text is layered across the image in a sans-serif type, reading "I SHOP THEREFORE I AM."

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

The image-text combination is direct: photograph plus overlaid words in the same frame. High contrast (black, white, red) and cropping make the composition feel like an ad you would see on a page or a screen. The pronoun "I" pulls the viewer into the sentence personally.

I · Interpret (what is it saying?)

The work rewrites a famous philosophical line, "I think therefore I am," to argue that consumer culture has replaced thought with buying as the thing that defines a person's identity.

J · Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds as persuasion because it is instantly readable and hard to forget. It stays on the responsible side of the propaganda line from Lesson 1: it argues against an idea (consumerism defining identity), not against a group of people, and it invites the viewer to think rather than simply obey.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH CRITIQUE STEP?
Read each statement about a social-commentary artwork. Decide which critique step it belongs to, then check it.
"The word 'YOUR' is printed larger than every other word, so it is the first thing the eye reads."
"This poster works because the image alone stops you, and the three-word slogan seals the point before you look away."
"A stenciled portrait fills most of the poster, with one short word printed below it in block capitals."
▶  In your notebook, write one Judge sentence about a Fairey poster before you open this. Then compare.
A strong Judge sentence points back to evidence. For example: "It succeeds because the stark portrait grabs attention in under a second, and the short slogan gives that attention a specific direction, without needing a paragraph of explanation." Your wording will differ; what matters is that your verdict rests on what you described and analyzed.
✍️ NOW YOU TRY
Choose whichever work pulls your eye more: Barbara Kruger's Untitled (I shop therefore I am) at the MoMA link above, or one of Shepard Fairey's posters at the OBEY Giant link above. In your notebook, run the same four steps, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, on the one you picked.
📝 VOCABULARY
AppropriationBorrowing an existing image or style and reusing it to make a new point.
Direct addressSpeaking straight to the viewer, often using "you" or "your," so the message feels personal.
Found imageAn existing photograph or picture an artist did not take but chooses to reuse.
Wheat-pastingA street-art method of gluing paper posters to public surfaces with a paste made from flour and water.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Kruger leads with words and lets the image set the trap; Fairey leads with image and lets a short slogan seal it. Different toolkits, same underlying craft: combine image, text, and audience on purpose, and take responsibility for the argument.
