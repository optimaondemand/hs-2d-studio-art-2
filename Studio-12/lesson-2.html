STUDIO 12 · LESSON 2
What Works?
Two painters who built meaning from pure color and shape in two very different ways. Then the critique method applied to work with no subject at all. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
When there is no subject to judge, how do you tell whether a non-objective painting actually works?
In Lesson 1 you met the difference between abstract and non-objective art. Now watch two painters solve the "no subject" problem in opposite ways, and practice the same four-step critique method you have used all year, this time with nothing recognizable to describe.
📚 PART 1 · TWO PAINTERS, ONE PROBLEM

Once a painter drops the subject, a new question appears: what decides where anything goes? Compare two painters who answered that question in almost opposite ways.

HF
Helen Frankenthaler
American, 1928–2011
THE EXPRESSIVE METHOD

Frankenthaler poured thinned oil paint onto raw, unprimed canvas laid on the floor, letting it soak into the fabric like a stain rather than sit on top like a layer of paint. The color spreads with soft, uneven edges, closer to a watermark than to anything planned in advance. Her Buzzard's Bay (1959) builds an entire non-objective composition from soaked color fields, no hard edges, no grid, just where the paint chose to go. This is the soak-stain technique, a founding move of what critics later called color-field painting.

WA
Wifredo Arcay
Cuban, 1925–1997
THE GEOMETRIC METHOD

Arcay worked in almost the opposite spirit. The 1953 Composition you will view is a screenprint his Paris studio, Atelier Arcay, made after Piet Mondrian's 1921 painting of the same name, and Arcay's own paintings belong to the same tradition: hard-edged geometric shapes, flat color, and careful, measured relationships between forms, closer to drafting than to pouring. Arcay was part of a generation of Latin American abstract artists who treated the canvas as a space to be planned and structured, not soaked and discovered. Where Frankenthaler let the paint move, this method decides exactly where every edge will sit.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Color-field painting is a mid-20th-century movement built from large, flat or soaked areas of color rather than lines or brush gesture. Frankenthaler's soak-stain technique, painting directly into raw canvas so the color becomes part of the fabric, is one of its most important inventions.
🔍 PART 2 · WATCH IT WORK

The critique method still works with no subject to name. Describe just gets harder, and more honest, because you cannot lean on "it's a tree." Here is the method applied to Kandinsky's Circles in a Circle from Lesson 1.

D · Describe (just the facts, no subject required)

Overlapping circles in red, blue, yellow, black, and white, crossed by straight bands and triangles. The largest circle sits slightly off-center. No recognizable object, figure, or landscape appears anywhere.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

Kandinsky uses rhythm (the repeated circles) and contrast (warm red and yellow against cool blue) to keep the eye moving. The diagonal bands cut against the circles, creating tension between curved and straight, calm and active.

I · Interpret (what is it saying, with no subject to point to?)

The painting feels like energy in motion, forms colliding and circling without ever settling. Kandinsky believed color and shape could carry feeling directly, the way a chord in music does, without needing to depict anything.

J · Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds because the relationships (warm against cool, curved against straight, large against small) create real visual energy without ever needing a subject to justify it. The elements alone do the work.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH STEP?
Read each statement about a non-objective painting. Decide which critique step it belongs to, then check it.
"The canvas is divided into three large soaked fields of blue, yellow, and rust orange."
"The soft, bleeding edges between the color fields create a feeling of one color dissolving into the next."
"The overlapping colors feel like the calm right before a storm, tense but not yet violent."
▶  In your notebook, write one Judge sentence about Frankenthaler's or Arcay's method before you open this. Then compare.
A strong Judge sentence points back to evidence. For example: "Frankenthaler's soak-stain succeeds because letting the paint move on its own creates soft, unforced color relationships that a planned, hard-edged approach like Arcay's could never produce, and that softness is exactly the feeling the work seems to want." Your wording will differ; what matters is that your verdict rests on what you described and analyzed, not just a preference.
✍️ NOW YOU TRY
Choose whichever work pulls your eye more: Kandinsky's Circles in a Circle from Lesson 1, Frankenthaler's Buzzard's Bay, or Arcay's Composition at the links above. In your notebook, run the same four steps, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, on the one you picked.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Frankenthaler let paint move; Arcay planned every edge. Opposite methods, and both non-objective, and both work. This critique method is how you judge a painting with no subject fairly: on its relationships, not on whether you can name what it shows.
