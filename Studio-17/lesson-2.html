STUDIO 17 · LESSON 2
What Synthesis Looks Like
There is no featured artist this Studio. The work under investigation is your own Studio 16 proposal. ~50 min
👀 PART 1 · LOOK AT YOUR OWN PLAN

Open your Studio 16 proposal again and read it slowly, the way you would read a masterwork you were about to critique. It contains three parts: research notes on an artist you chose, sketches exploring the idea, and a written proposal for an original project. Today those three parts get named honestly, the way a critique names any work, so you know exactly what you are building this Studio.

💬 WONDER FIRST
Most students are harder critics of other people's work than their own. Before you continue, ask yourself one honest question: if a classmate handed you this exact proposal, what would you tell them their biggest risk is?
🔍 PART 2 · CRITIQUE, APPLIED TO A PLAN INSTEAD OF A PICTURE

You have used this four-step critique method all year on finished artworks. It works just as well on a plan, because a strong plan is really a description of a work that does not exist yet. Read your proposal against each step.

D · Describe (what does the proposal actually say?)

State it in one or two plain sentences: what medium, what subject or idea, what size, what is being made. No opinions yet, just what the proposal literally commits to.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

Which Elements and Principles does your own plan lean on? Which technique from an earlier Studio does it ask you to use at a higher level than before? Name them specifically, the way you would name them in someone else's work.

I · Interpret (what is it trying to say?)

What idea, feeling, or question is this piece actually about? If you cannot answer this in one sentence, that is useful information now, while there is still time to sharpen it, not after you have committed twenty hours to the wrong idea.

J · Judge (is it realistic, as written?)

Judge it the way a teacher would judge feasibility, not just ambition: can this actually be finished, well, at this scale, in the time this Studio allows? This is the honest question the rest of this lesson exists to help you answer.

⚡ TRY IT · DESCRIBE, ANALYZE, INTERPRET, OR JUDGE?
Each statement below could appear in a student's self-review of their own proposal. Decide which critique step it belongs to, then check it.
"Three panels at this level of detail will not fit in the time I have, so I am cutting to two."
"My proposal is a two-panel mixed-media piece, about 18 by 24 inches each, on the theme of memory."
"I am relying on layered collage and torn edges to build texture across both panels."
📚 PART 3 · "LARGE-SCALE" AND "SELF-DIRECTED," HONESTLY

The Studio Project spec asks for a large-scale, self-directed artwork from the approved proposal. Both words carry real weight, and it is worth being honest with yourself about what each one is actually asking.

LARGE-SCALE MEANS

Bigger and more demanding than your normal two-week Studio project: more surface, more panels, more layers, or more hours of sustained work than you have given any single piece this year.

LARGE-SCALE DOES NOT MEAN

Expensive materials, a size that requires special equipment, or a subject so large in ambition that it cannot realistically finish. Scale should serve the idea, not overwhelm it.

SELF-DIRECTED MEANS

You are the one deciding the next step at every stage: what to fix, what to push further, when a section is done. The teacher grades the result; you run the process.

SELF-DIRECTED DOES NOT MEAN

Unplanned. Self-direction without a plan usually stalls halfway through. The milestones you build in Lesson 3 are what make self-direction sustainable instead of chaotic.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
If Lesson 2's honest critique turns up a real problem with your proposal (the scope is too big, the idea is too vague, the medium will not hold up), that is not a failure. Studios revise proposals before finished work far more than they revise finished work after the fact. Flag the change and bring it into Lesson 3's planning.

➡️ AFTER THIS LESSON
1
Write your one-sentence Judge statement · before Lesson 3
In your notebook, finish this sentence: "As written, my proposal is realistic / needs one change, which is ___." Bring it into Lesson 3.
2
Continue to Lesson 3: Planning a Large Work
Turn your reviewed proposal into thumbnails, milestones, materials, and a time budget.
💪 THE BIG IDEA

The same critical method that reads a masterwork can read your own plan. Describe it, analyze it, interpret it, and judge it honestly before you spend weeks building it. A little criticism now saves a great deal of frustration later.
