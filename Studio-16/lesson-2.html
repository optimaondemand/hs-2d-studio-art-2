STUDIO 16 · LESSON 2
Researching an Artist Responsibly
Study one artist the way a real researcher does: sourced, verified, and cited. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How do you know what you know about an artist, and how sure can you be that it is true?
Anyone can repeat a fact half-remembered from a documentary or a skimmed caption. A researcher can point to where a fact came from. This lesson teaches you to research the way a museum curator or art historian does, using the sources this course has cited all year, so your Capstone proposal stands on solid ground.
📚 PART 1 · TWO KINDS OF EVIDENCE

Art researchers sort what they find into two categories. Knowing the difference tells you how much weight to put on a claim.

📜 PRIMARY SOURCES

  • The artwork itself, viewed directly or through a museum's high-resolution image
  • The artist's own letters, journals, interviews, or artist statements
  • A museum's official collection record (title, date, medium, dimensions, provenance)
  • Original exhibition catalogs from the artist's lifetime

📚 SECONDARY SOURCES

  • A scholarly essay or museum education page analyzing the artist's work
  • An encyclopedia entry (Britannica, Grove Art Online) summarizing a career
  • A documentary or Smarthistory/Khan Academy video walking through a piece
  • A peer-reviewed article accessed through a database such as JSTOR
💡 USE BOTH, ON PURPOSE
Secondary sources are usually where you start; they orient you to an artist's life and significance efficiently. Primary sources are where you confirm the specific facts you plan to state as true, especially dates, titles, and quotations. A strong research note names which kind of source backs each claim.
✅ PART 2 · VERIFYING A FACT

A fact is verified when you can point to a specific, credible source for it, and ideally a second source agrees. This is exactly what the critique method has trained you to do all year: describe only what you can actually support, not what you assume.

Step 1 Find the claim

You read that an artist "was rejected by the Salon in 1863." That is a claim, not yet a fact you can use.

Step 2 Trace it to a source

Where did you read it? A museum collection page, a Smarthistory essay, a Britannica entry? Note the specific page, not just "the internet."

Step 3 Cross-check if it matters

For a fact you plan to state confidently in your proposal, find it in a second credible source. If two independent sources agree, treat it as verified.

Step 4 Cite it

Record the source with your notes: artist, work title and date, source name, and a link or catalog reference. Your research notes submission is exactly this record.

⚠️ A HONEST DEBATE IS NOT A FAILED FACT
Sometimes scholars genuinely disagree, on an attribution, a date, or an artist's intent. If your sources disagree, say so in your notes rather than picking whichever version is more convenient. "Sources disagree on the exact date; the Met catalogs it as circa 1888, while an earlier biography lists 1887" is itself a well-researched, honest sentence.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI FOR RESEARCH VS. MAKING THE ART
An AI chat tool can summarize an artist's career quickly, and that can be a fast way to get oriented before you dig into real sources. But AI tools sometimes state dates, titles, or attributions confidently and get them wrong, and they cannot show you where a claim came from the way a museum record or a cited essay can. For this Studio: you may use AI to help you brainstorm search terms or get a quick overview of an unfamiliar artist, but every fact in your research notes must be traced back to a verifiable source (the approved list in Lesson 3), not left resting on an AI summary alone. The sketches, thesis, and proposal writing are your own thinking; that is what the assignment grades.
⚡ TRY IT · SOURCE OR NOT YET SOURCED?
Decide whether each research move counts as a verified, cited fact or still needs more work.
"I read on a random fan blog that the painting was finished in one afternoon."
"The Met's online collection record and a Smarthistory essay both give the same date and medium for the work."
"An AI chatbot told me the artist's most famous quote, so I wrote it in my notes as fact."
📝 VOCABULARY
Primary sourceFirsthand evidence: the artwork, the artist's own words, or an official museum record.
Secondary sourceSomeone else's researched account or analysis of the artist or work.
Verified factA claim traced to a credible source, ideally confirmed by a second independent source.
CitationA record of where a fact came from: source name, and a link or catalog reference.
AttributionThe recognized authorship of a work; sometimes debated among scholars.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

A researched fact can be traced to its source. An assumed fact cannot. The difference is not talent or intelligence, it is one habit: always ask "how do I know this?" before you write it down.
