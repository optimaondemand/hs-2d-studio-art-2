STUDIO 6 · LESSON 6
Refine, Reflect & Present
A strong collage is edited, not just assembled. Critique your own layers, then finish and present. ~55 min + studio time
🔄 PART 1 · EDIT BEFORE THE GLUE DRIES FOR GOOD

Set your collage at arm's length, or better, across the room. Most collages are not finished on the first pass. They are finished on the second look, when the maker notices one scrap that is not pulling its weight.

👀 STEP BACK AND SQUINT
From across the room, does your eye still find one clear focal point, or does everything now compete equally? Is there any scrap that, if you covered it with your hand, the piece would actually improve? If so, that scrap is decoration, not composition, and a strong final pass often means adding one small accent rather than removing anything already glued.
🔍 PART 2 · CRITIQUE YOUR OWN WORK

Before you call it done, critique your own collage using the same method you used on Höch and Bearden in Lesson 2. Be your own first critic.

D · Describe

What materials are actually in your piece? List them plainly: fabric, magazine text, a photograph, patterned paper.

A · Analyze

Check your tools. Is there a clear shared thread (shape, color, or scale) connecting your scraps? Is there one obvious focal point? Are your edges (torn, cut, hidden) a choice you can name, not an accident?

I · Interpret

Does the piece communicate your chosen memory to someone who was not there? What might a stranger misread, and does that matter?

J · Judge

What one change would most improve it? Usually it is strengthening the shared thread or adding one accent to sharpen the focal point. Make that change before you submit.

💭 REVISION CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
You step back and your collage looks scattered, not unified. What is the most likely fix?
📝 PART 3 · WRITE YOUR ARTIST STATEMENT

Your artist statement travels with the artwork. It is two or three focused sentences, in your own voice, that show you made deliberate choices. Type it into the submission text box with your collage.

A strong statement answers three things:

  • What memory or experience did you choose, and why does it matter to you?
  • Which collage tools did you use on purpose, and where? (Name at least two: juxtaposition, real vs. implied texture, layering, edge choices.)
  • What did a viewer notice in your piece that surprised you, or what would you do differently next time?
▶  Draft your three sentences in your notebook first, then open this for a sample of the register (do not copy it).
"I built this collage around the memory of my grandmother's kitchen, using a scrap of floral fabric that matched her actual tablecloth as my base layer. I used real texture (the fabric) layered under implied texture (drawn steam lines) to suggest warmth, and I left my top layer's edges torn on purpose so the memory would feel handled, not polished. I was surprised how much the smell of that kitchen came back to me just from choosing the right color of paper." Your statement should be this specific, but about your memory and your choices.

✍️ DO NOT FORGET
Every finished artwork carries its artist's name. Sign your collage, ideally in a spot that does not compete with your focal point. A signature is how you say: this is mine, I made it, I stand behind it.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

You began Studio 6 with a pile of scraps that, on their own, meant nothing you intended. Two weeks of choosing, juxtaposing, and layering turned them into one argument about a memory that matters to you. That is the whole craft of collage: meaning built between the pieces, never inside just one of them.
