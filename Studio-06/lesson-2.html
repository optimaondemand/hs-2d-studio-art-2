STUDIO 6 · LESSON 2
The Cut and the Glue
Three artists who built new meaning out of scissors, paste, and a refusal to draw from scratch. ~50 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
What can scissors and glue say that a pencil cannot?
In Lesson 1 you met juxtaposition: meaning built between pieces rather than inside a single drawn mark. Now watch three artists who made that idea their whole practice. Because all three works are under copyright, you will view them at their museum sources rather than here.
👀 PART 1 · HANNAH HÖCH'S CUT-UP WORLD
HH
Hannah Höch
German, 1889–1978
DADA PHOTOMONTAGE PIONEER

Höch was one of the earliest artists to build finished, exhibited work entirely from cut photographs and printed matter, magazines, newspapers, advertisements, sliced apart and reassembled into new bodies and new scenes. Working in Berlin in the years after the First World War, she used photomontage to question fixed ideas about gender, machines, and modern life, often by combining human features with mechanical or architectural fragments.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the museum site) VERIFY URL AT DEPLOY
Hannah Höch, Das Sternenfilet (The Star Net), 1924

Höch's work is under copyright, so we view it at the source rather than reproduce it here. Look for how cut photographic fragments of faces and bodies are reassembled at odd scales and angles, refusing to add up to one natural figure. Notice where an edge is left deliberately visible, a torn or cut line the artist does not try to hide.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Dada was an art movement that began around 1916 in reaction to the First World War. Dada artists used chance, absurdity, and found materials, rather than traditional drawing or painting, partly to protest a world that felt senselessly destroyed and partly to question whether "fine art" still meant anything. Höch was one of the few women given real recognition inside the mostly male Berlin Dada group.
📚 PART 2 · KURT SCHWITTERS AND "MERZ"
KS
Kurt Schwitters
German, 1887–1948
ART FROM DISCARDED SCRAPS

Schwitters built his own personal art movement, which he named Merz, out of a single fragment of a word he cut from a piece of found paper. Merz collages are built almost entirely from what most people would throw away: bus tickets, wrappers, torn labels, scraps of wood and cloth, string, and cancelled stamps. He believed literally any material could carry artistic value once it was placed with care.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the museum site) VERIFY URL AT DEPLOY
Kurt Schwitters, Mz collages, 1921

Schwitters's work is under copyright, so we view it at the source rather than reproduce it here. Look closely at the range of found materials in one work: how many different textures, papers, and printed fragments can you count? Notice how the composition still follows a clear sense of balance and arrangement even though every scrap was found, not made.

🎭 PART 3 · ROMARE BEARDEN'S LAYERED LIFE
RB
Romare Bearden
American, 1911–1988
COLLAGE OF BLACK AMERICAN LIFE

Bearden turned to collage in the 1960s as the clearest way to show the layered, fragmented, and richly textured experience of Black American life, drawing on his own childhood memories of North Carolina, Pittsburgh, and Harlem. He combined cut photographs, painted paper, and patterned fabric into scenes of everyday community: musicians, families, front porches, church, and city streets, all built from many separate pieces fused into one composition.

🔗 VIEW THE WORK (opens the museum site) VERIFY URL AT DEPLOY
Romare Bearden, The Soul Three, 1968

Bearden's work is under copyright, so we view it at the source rather than reproduce it here. Look for how photographic fragments (often faces or hands) are combined with flat painted color and patterned paper. Notice the scale relationships: does every fragment share one consistent sense of size, or does Bearden let some pieces stay a different scale on purpose?

💬 WONDER FIRST
Höch cut up photographs to question identity. Schwitters rescued literal trash to build a whole movement. Bearden layered photographs, paint, and fabric to tell the story of a community. Three artists, three very different reasons to reach for scissors instead of a pencil. Which reason feels closest to the memory you might build your own project around?
🔍 PART 4 · WATCH IT WORK · CRITIQUE ON BEARDEN

Remember the order from earlier Studios: Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge. You cannot skip ahead. Here is the critique method applied to The Soul Three.

D · Describe (just the facts)

Three figures are built from a combination of cut photographic fragments (especially faces and hands), flat painted color, and pieces of patterned paper or fabric, all arranged into one grouped scene.

A · Analyze (how is it built?)

Bearden mixes at least three different kinds of material (photograph, paint, pattern) in a single composition, and controls unity through repeated color and a shared grouping of the three figures, even though every piece came from a different source.

I · Interpret (what is it saying?)

The layered, pieced-together construction mirrors the layered, pieced-together nature of memory and community itself: no single clean photograph could tell this story, but many fragments arranged together can.

J · Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds because the technique matches the subject. A collage of many small truths captures communal life more honestly than one polished, single-source image could.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH CRITIQUE STEP?
Read each statement about a collage. Decide which critique step it belongs to, then check it.
"The image combines torn newspaper text, a photograph of a hand, and a strip of striped fabric."
"The largest scrap sits dead center and every other piece is angled so the eye is pulled toward it first."
"Combining a torn family photograph with a scrap of wallpaper suggests that a home is built as much from ordinary, unremarkable materials as from the people inside it."
▶  In your notebook, write one Judge sentence about Höch's Das Sternenfilet before you open this. Then compare.
A strong Judge sentence points back to evidence. For example: "It succeeds because the visibly cut, mismatched edges refuse to let the viewer settle into one comfortable read of the figure, which forces the same unsettled questioning of identity that Höch intended." Your wording will differ; what matters is that your verdict rests on what you described and analyzed.
✍️ NOW YOU TRY
Choose whichever work pulls your eye more: Höch's Das Sternenfilet, Schwitters's Mz collages, or Bearden's The Soul Three. In your notebook, run the same four steps, describe, analyze, interpret, judge, on the one you picked.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Höch, Schwitters, and Bearden each proved that a scrap headed for the trash could carry as much meaning as anything drawn by hand, once it was chosen, placed, and made to speak beside its neighbors.
