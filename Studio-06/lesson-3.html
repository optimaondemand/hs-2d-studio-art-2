STUDIO 6 · LESSON 3
Juxtaposition & Composition
Any three scraps can sit on a page. Only a composed arrangement reads as one idea. ~50 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Choose disparate scraps that create a strong juxtaposition, then arrange them so the composition reads as unified rather than scattered. Meaning depends on both halves: the surprise of the pairing, and the discipline of the arrangement.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT MAKES A JUXTAPOSITION WORK

Not every pairing of unrelated scraps produces meaning. A strong juxtaposition usually has one thing in common across otherwise different materials, a shared color, a shared shape, a shared scale, or a shared subject, while everything else about the two pieces clashes. That single shared thread is what lets the viewer's eye connect them instead of just seeing two random scraps.

Shared shape, clashing materialMary Delany built this flower from hundreds of separate paper scraps in different colors and finishes. What unifies it is the repeated petal and leaf shape carried through every piece, no two of which match in material.
Mary Delany, Parnassia palustris paper mosaic, 1776. Wikimedia Commons, public domain (artist died 1788).
Shared scale, clashing subjectQuilters have always solved this problem: every fabric square here is a totally different printed pattern, but every square is the exact same size and shape, so the whole quilt reads as one unified surface.
Courthouse Square quilt, Northern Illinois, 1849, Wisconsin Historical Museum. Wikimedia Commons, CC0.
Shared color, clashing scaleNo single collage image shows this exact case, so here is the closest real parallel: a Roman mosaic maker had the same problem, thousands of small stone tesserae of very different sizes, held together as one image by a small, repeated color palette.
Ancient Roman geometric mosaic, Palazzo Massimo alle Terme, Rome. Photo by Mbellaccini, edited by Chiswick Chap. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.
💡 ONE THREAD, MANY CONTRASTS
Think of it as one rope tying a pile of mismatched objects together. The rope (shared shape, scale, or color) does not need to be obvious to the viewer at first glance, but it needs to exist, or the eye has nothing to hold onto and the page reads as clutter rather than composition.
✍️ PART 2 · GUIDED PRACTICE (do this now)

Pull three scraps from the pile you started gathering in Lesson 1. They do not need to be perfect yet; this is a practice arrangement, not the final project.

  1. Lay all three out loose (2 min). Do not glue anything yet. Spread the three scraps across your page and just look.
  2. Find the one shared thread (3 min). Look for a shape, color, or scale that at least two of the three scraps already share, even faintly. If none exists, try overlapping or trimming one scrap until it does.
  3. Arrange for a focal point (5 min). Decide which one scrap should draw the eye first. Give it the most contrast (largest, darkest, or most centrally placed) and let the other two support it rather than compete with it.
  4. Test by squinting (1 min). Squint at your loose arrangement. If it reads as one shape with variety inside it, rather than three separate shapes, the composition is working.
⚡ TRY IT · UNIFIED OR SCATTERED?
Read each arrangement description. Decide whether it is likely to read as unified or scattered, then check it.
Three scraps of totally different colors, totally different sizes, and totally different shapes, spaced evenly apart with no overlap.
Three scraps in different materials but the same warm orange-red family, overlapping slightly, with one clearly larger and more central than the others.
🖉️ SKILL BUILDER SKETCH (turn in this week)

Three-Scrap Unity Study

Arrange (and glue down, or photograph before gluing) three unrelated scraps on one small page so they read as a single unified composition through one shared thread: shape, color, or scale. Write one sentence identifying your shared thread and one sentence identifying your focal point. This is a completion-graded composition drill, not the final Studio Project.

Full directions and submission steps are in the Canvas assignment Skill Builder 1: Three-Scrap Unity Study. Photograph your page and upload it, then save a copy to your OneDrive portfolio folder.

📝 VOCABULARY
JuxtapositionPlacing unrelated things side by side so each changes how the other is read.
UnityThe sense that separate parts of a composition belong together as one whole.
Focal pointThe area of a composition the eye is drawn to first.
Shared threadA repeated shape, color, or scale connecting otherwise different materials.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Surprise alone is not composition. A strong collage needs one thread, a shape, a color, a scale, that a viewer's eye can follow through the clash, turning scattered scraps into a single held idea.
