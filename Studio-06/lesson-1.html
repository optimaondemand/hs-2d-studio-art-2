STUDIO 6 · LESSON 1
Scraps That Speak
This Studio builds meaning out of things that, on their own, meant something else entirely. ~45 min
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How can torn scraps of unrelated things add up to a single meaning?
Every Studio so far has asked you to draw or paint a mark. This one asks you to build with pieces that already existed somewhere else: a newspaper headline, a ticket stub, a scrap of wallpaper, a photograph cut from a magazine. Collage does not invent its materials. It finds new meaning by placing old materials next to each other on purpose.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Before any names or dates, look at a stage the way an artist building a collage sees it: separate pieces, waiting to be placed. Nothing here was drawn. It was gathered, cut, and arranged.

A mockup collage stage: seven unrelated scraps, torn and overlapped at different angles, before any single meaning has been chosen.

On its own, each scrap above is nothing: a torn corner, a shape, a strip of text. None of them were made to go together. But the moment an artist starts overlapping them, choosing what sits on top of what, and where the eye lands first, the pile stops being scraps and becomes a composition that says one thing.

💬 WONDER FIRST
Look again at the mockup. Which two scraps, if placed directly beside each other, would create the strangest or most interesting relationship? Why do you think that pairing catches your eye more than the others? Hold that thought. The artists in Lesson 2 built entire careers on exactly that instinct.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Have you ever kept a physical scrap (a ticket, a note, a photo, a piece of fabric) just because of what it reminded you of?
📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

Collage (from the French coller, "to glue") is art built by gluing or arranging existing materials, paper, fabric, photographs, printed text, onto a surface. When those materials include photographs or printed images specifically, the technique is often called photomontage. Either way, the artist is not inventing new marks so much as finding new relationships between marks that already existed.

The engine that makes collage work is juxtaposition: placing two or more things side by side so that each changes how you read the other. A photograph of an eye alone is just an eye. That same eye pasted onto a mechanical gear, at the scale of a machine part, becomes a comment on how humans and machines are starting to look alike. Neither piece changed. Only their relationship did.

📄 A SCRAP ALONE

  • Carries its original, single meaning
  • A headline is just a headline
  • A photograph is just a photograph
  • No relationship to argue with
  • The viewer's eye has nowhere new to go

🔄 THE SAME SCRAP, JUXTAPOSED

  • Gains a second, new meaning from its neighbor
  • A headline beside a torn photo becomes commentary
  • Scale, texture, and material clash on purpose
  • The relationship is the artwork
  • The viewer's eye moves and compares
THE ONE IDEA OF THIS STUDIO
Meaning is not glued onto the page.
It is built between the pieces.
Every technique in the next five lessons is a way of controlling what happens in the gap where two unrelated scraps meet.

Collage of this kind has deep roots in Dada, an early-20th-century art movement that used chance, absurdity, and found materials to protest the destruction of the First World War and question whether traditional "fine art" still made sense. Cutting up newspapers and gluing them back together in disordered ways was, for the Dadaists, a serious act: it mirrored a world that felt cut up and reassembled wrong. Meet four terms now that you will use in every lesson this Studio.

CollageArt built from existing materials arranged and glued onto a surface.
PhotomontageCollage built specifically from cut and combined photographs or printed images.
JuxtapositionPlacing unrelated things side by side so each changes how the other is read.
Mixed mediaArtwork combining more than one material or technique in a single piece.
🔍 PART 3 · WHY THIS COMES NOW

Every Studio before this one asked you to make marks from scratch: drawn lines, mixed colors, invented space. Studio 6 asks something different: can you compose meaning out of materials you did not make? This matters because most images a person encounters daily, in ads, in the news, on a screen, were not made by that person either. Arranging found material with intention is learning to read, and eventually control, that visual language.

Your project for these two weeks is called Memory in Layers. You will build a mixed-media collage that communicates one personal memory or experience, using paper, magazine scraps, found textures, and glue to say something a single drawn image could not say alone.

▶  Before you read on, name one object you could tear or cut a small piece from right now (a receipt, an envelope, a wrapper). Then open this.
Almost anything qualifies. That is exactly the point of collage: the raw material is already all around you, in forms most people throw away without a second look. Start a small pile of scraps this week, magazine pages, ticket stubs, patterned paper, fabric swatches, receipts. You will need more material than you think, because most collage artists use only a fraction of what they gather.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Nothing in a collage is invented from nothing. Every scrap already meant something before the artist touched it. The art is in the choosing and the placing: deciding which old meanings will collide to make a new one.
