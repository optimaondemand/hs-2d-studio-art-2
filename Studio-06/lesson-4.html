STUDIO 6 · LESSON 4
Real vs. Implied Texture
Collage can give a viewer an actual surface to imagine touching, not just a picture of one. ~50 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
Tell the difference between real texture and implied texture, use layering to build depth, and control your edges on purpose, deciding when to hide a seam and when to leave it visible.
📚 PART 1 · TWO KINDS OF TEXTURE

Painters and drawers can only give you implied texture: marks that make a surface look rough, smooth, or fuzzy, without the paper actually changing under your fingertip. Collage is one of the few techniques that can also give a viewer real texture, an actual physical surface (corrugated cardboard, woven fabric, crumpled foil) glued right onto the page.

Implied textureEvery ridge and shadow on this beetle is built from drawn ink lines. The paper itself stays perfectly flat and smooth; the texture exists only in the eye.
Razia Baksh, Beetle ink drawing. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.
Real textureThis ridged surface is not drawn. It is an actual physical material that casts a real, tiny shadow and would change under a fingertip, exactly what a scrap of cardboard adds to a collage.
Richard Wheeler (Zephyris), Corrugated Cardboard. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.
Combining bothNo single photograph shows a finished combination, since that is exactly what you build yourself. This crumpled, torn paper is a real base texture like the ones collage artists glue down; drawn implied-texture marks added on top of a scrap like this is the move that gives collage a range no painting alone can match.
Pink Sherbet Photography (D Sharon Pruitt), Torn Ripped Wrinkled Damaged Paper. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0.
💡 WHY COMBINE THEM
Real texture on its own can feel like a display of materials rather than an artwork. Drawn marks on top of a real textured scrap tie it back into the rest of your composition and let you push emphasis exactly where you want it, on top of a surface no drawing could invent alone.
✍️ PART 2 · LAYERING AND THE HONEST EDGE

Layering means overlapping scraps so some sit visibly on top of others, building physical depth the way overlap builds depth in a drawing (Studio 4). Every overlap creates an edge, the line where one scrap ends and another begins, and every edge is a decision.

  1. Torn edge. Tearing (rather than cutting) leaves a rough, irregular line. It reads as raw, honest, and handmade, and it is the edge Höch and Schwitters both used constantly.
  2. Cut edge. A scissor or blade cut is crisp and controlled. It reads as deliberate and graphic, useful when you want a shape to feel intentional rather than found.
  3. Hidden edge. Overlapping, blending colors at the seam, or matching a pattern across two scraps can make an edge nearly disappear, letting two different materials read as one continuous surface.
  4. Choose on purpose. Before you glue anything down, decide: does this seam want to be seen, or hidden? Both are valid. The mistake is an edge that is accidental rather than chosen.

🖉️ SKILL BUILDER SKETCH (turn in this week)

Layered Texture Sample

On one small page, layer at least three scraps that overlap, using at least one real-texture material and at least one area of drawn implied texture. For each overlap, choose an edge on purpose (torn, cut, or hidden) and label which you chose and why in one sentence per edge. This is a completion-graded technique drill, not the final Studio Project.

Full directions and submission steps are in the Canvas assignment Skill Builder 2: Layered Texture Sample. Photograph your page, upload it, and save a copy to your OneDrive portfolio folder.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Collage can give a viewer a texture no drawing can fake, but only if every layer and every edge is a choice. An accidental seam reads as sloppy; a chosen one reads as intention, whether it is hidden smooth or left honestly torn.
